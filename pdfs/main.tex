\begin{abstract}
The integration of European electricity markets, the proliferation of cross-border interconnections, and the rising frequency of extreme operational stress (heat waves, renewable droughts, cascading failures) are turning power-system flexibility studies into a \emph{scenario-exploration} problem: analysts must evaluate large catalogues of what-if cases under tight per-scenario compute budgets. Mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) gives rigorous, auditable answers but does not scale to such catalogues. We propose a hybrid \textbf{MILP--GNN--Latent EBM} stack that recasts unit-commitment and dispatch as a \emph{compute-bounded} exploration process, generating a distribution of feasibility-certified candidates within a controllable wall-clock budget. The stack chains a diversity-maximising scenario generator with a $22$-axis ex-ante criticality index, a multi-layer MILP oracle for labels, a heterogeneous temporal graph encoder, a JEPA latent predictor with a conditional Langevin-sampled Energy-Based Model, and a cascading LP worker that certifies each candidate as a primal-feasible plan. Benchmarked against an unconstrained MILP oracle and a rolling-horizon merit-order heuristic (RH-MO+LP) over $300$ stratified and $300$ persona scenarios, three findings emerge: (i) \emph{compute-bounded exploration is supported} -- the pipeline is the only solver with non-trivial sub-second feasibility, with a strictly positive pipeline-only-feasible share in every family; (ii) the matched-budget advantage is \emph{statistically predictable} from criticality drivers ($70.7\%$ cross-validated accuracy vs a $31\%$ base rate); and (iii) \emph{scaling with criticality is not supported} -- the median cost gap to MILP-$120\,\mathrm{s}$ grows from $0.23\%$ (low) to $129\%$ (high). A preliminary $N{-}1$ contingency study further shows that commitment robustness is governed by \emph{global} co-optimisation: the full MILP stays dispatch-feasible under every single-line outage across all criticality bands, whereas partial and learned commitments are feasible on all low-criticality cases but on only $33\%$ of high-criticality ones, over-committing thermal units beyond what load-shedding can absorb. The stack is therefore a fast exploration engine that complements rather than replaces the MILP oracle, with criticality-based routing dispatching each scenario to its preferred solver.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Power System; Multi-Scale Modelling; MILP; Graph Neural Networks; Energy-Based Models; Scenario Exploration.

\section{Introduction}

Power systems are undergoing a profound transformation, driven by the large-scale integration of variable renewable energy (VRE), rapid electrification of end uses, and the growing penetration of distributed energy resources (DER) such as demand response (DR) and storage. This transition pushes system operators and planners to mobilize flexibility across \emph{multiple spatial layers} (from behind-the-meter assets to regional and national transmission systems) and \emph{multiple temporal scales} (from intra-hour balancing to seasonal adequacy), while guaranteeing feasibility under network, market, and security constraints. Two converging trends amplify this challenge. On the structural side, the progressive integration of European electricity markets---toward a single wholesale price zone with dense cross-border interconnections---is producing power systems of unprecedented spatial scale that must be co-optimised across hundreds of zones and multiple voltage levels. On the operational side, the increasing frequency of black-swan events (extreme heat waves, prolonged Dunkelflaute episodes, cascading equipment failures) creates urgent demand for near-real-time solving of large-scale unit-commitment and economic-dispatch problems under stress conditions that lie far outside historical experience.

A central methodological challenge is that the questions stakeholders increasingly ask are not single-run questions (``what is the optimal schedule today?''), but \emph{interactive what-if questions} that require exploring thousands of scenarios: extreme weather and correlated forecast errors, congestion and topology changes, new flexibility portfolios (storage and DR), and coupled infrastructures (e.g., gas--power--carbon interactions). In these settings, the practical bottleneck is not only solving one high-fidelity instance, but enabling \emph{near-real-time scenario exploration} where users can turn a ``complexity knob'' (more spatial detail, longer horizons, tighter constraints, more uncertainty) and still obtain fast, credible answers.

Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) provides a rigorous and auditable representation of these decisions, and remains the reference tool for high-fidelity scheduling and planning \parencite{conejo_investment_2016}. However, repeated MILP solves become rapidly prohibitive as scenario count and model complexity increase, due to non-convexities (e.g., unit commitment logic) and strong inter-temporal and network couplings. Conversely, learning-based surrogates---and in particular Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)---can exploit grid topology to deliver fast predictions, but purely predictive approaches can violate hard constraints, drift out of distribution, and lose economic meaning.

This paper proposes a hybrid \textbf{MILP--GNN--Latent EBM} methodology for multi-scale flexibility exploration that turns high-fidelity optimisation into a compute-bounded exploration engine. Concretely, we develop an end-to-end pipeline that couples synthetic scenario generation, a multi-layer MILP oracle, a topology-consistent graph representation, a learned latent space over commitment strategies, and feasibility-preserving post-processing.

Rather than replacing optimisation, the proposed surrogate stack aims to preserve MILP-level structure while enabling \emph{compute-bounded} scenario sweeps. The MILP oracle produces ground-truth labels; a Hierarchical Temporal Encoder (HTE) compresses each scenario into a topology-consistent embedding; a UC autoencoder maps the binary commitment matrix into a low-dimensional strategy latent; a JEPA predictor maps each scenario embedding to an anchor in that latent space; a conditional Latent Energy-Based Model (Latent EBM) and a cosine-annealed Langevin sampler explore the strategy latent around the anchor; finally, a feasibility decoder and a cascading LP worker turn each latent candidate into a primal-feasible dispatch plan. The objective is not to predict a single optimum but to enable fast and controlled exploration of feasibility-certified candidates inside a tight wall-clock budget. The relative performance of this stack is then characterised against two reference solvers under matched protocols, with regimes and failure modes identified empirically rather than asserted.


\subsection{Literature Review}

MILP remains the workhorse for operational scheduling (e.g., unit commitment and multi-period dispatch) and long-term planning under uncertainty, due to its ability to encode discrete decisions, operational constraints, and economic objectives in a transparent manner \parencite{conejo_investment_2016}. In expansion planning, stochastic and multi-stage MILP formulations capture uncertainty in costs and renewable availability, but typically require decomposition, scenario reduction, or aggregation to remain tractable \parencite{micheli_two-stage_2023}. Beyond single-level optimization, multi-level and equilibrium-aware formulations have been developed to represent strategic interactions and sequential market clearings: early MPEC-based approaches model strategic bidding under network constraints \parencite{hobbs_strategic_2000}, while three-level MILP formulations integrate transmission expansion, generation investment, and market equilibrium at the operational layer \parencite{pozo_three-level_2013}. More recently, scalable approximations for mixed-integer multi-level problems with sequential followers have been proposed, together with dedicated Benders-type decomposition strategies to address computational scaling in coupled electricity--gas--carbon market settings \parencite{xia_scalable_2025}.

Building on these MILP foundations, when flexibility is distributed across buildings, districts, and regions, MILP models can explicitly represent coordination across spatial layers, but the number of integer variables and coupling constraints grows rapidly with resolution and horizon length. Holarchic or hierarchical formulations mitigate this growth through structured aggregation and decomposition, enabling urban- or district-scale optimization while preserving key network interactions \parencite{marquant_holarchic_2017}. Robust hierarchical MILP designs further address uncertainty (e.g., demand ranges) via min--max constructs, at the cost of additional layers and higher computational burden \parencite{yokoyama_hierarchical_2021}. Overall, the literature shows a consistent tension: high-fidelity multi-layer MILPs are auditable and economically meaningful, but they remain expensive for large scenario sweeps and interactive exploration.

To alleviate this computational bottleneck, recent work has explored GNNs as topology-aware surrogates or accelerators. Early work showed that graph neural architectures can emulate load-flow/physics solvers and generalize across topologies \parencite{donon_graph_2019}, and can approximate OPF solutions via imitation learning on AC settings \parencite{owerko_optimal_2019}. For AC optimal power flow (AC-OPF), topology-informed GNNs have been designed with feasibility regularization and adaptation mechanisms for topology contingencies \parencite{liu_topology-aware_2023}. Complementary approaches use GNNs to provide warm-starts or initial estimates, reducing the iteration count of interior-point solvers and thereby accelerating high-fidelity OPF computations \parencite{deihim_initial_2024}. Other studies benchmark GNN architectures (e.g., attention-based) for efficient AC power flow or OPF prediction in larger grids \parencite{talebi_graph_2025}. Beyond OPF, GNN proxies have been trained from large collections of MILP solves to support hours-ahead risk assessment under evolving topology, with reported gains in speed and situational awareness \parencite{zhang_graph_2024}. In restoration and UC-like settings, learning-based policies have also been proposed to accelerate MILP-based decision making, e.g., for load restoration \parencite{ahmed_advancing_2024}, by predicting an initial commitment refined via large neighborhood search \parencite{yang_solving_nodate}, or by directly learning branching/diving policies in modern MIP solvers \parencite{qin_solve_2023}. Related hybrid strategies combine GNN predictions with LP relaxations to fix stable variables and accelerate UC while controlling suboptimality \parencite{yang_stable_2025}, and confidence-aware architectures seed commitment and active-constraint predictions to speed up large industrial RAC formulations \parencite{park_confidence-aware_2023}. At the planning level, GNN surrogates have been proposed to approximate the outputs of MILP energy system models, enabling faster what-if analyses \parencite{pjetri_graph_nodate}.

A persistent challenge in learning-to-optimize is enforcing hard constraints and providing controlled exploration of complex discrete spaces. Two complementary strands are particularly relevant: (i) learning to make discrete decisions inside MILP solvers, and (ii) learning generative/energy-based models for exploration.

On the solver side, MILPs admit a natural bipartite constraint--variable graph representation that can be exploited by GNN policies for branching decisions \parencite{gasse_exact_2019}. However, fundamental representational limits also exist: there are MILP families that all GNNs will treat equally unless additional structure or features are injected \parencite{chen_representing_2023}, motivating hybrid designs where learning is guided by optimization structure.

On the generative side, energy-based formulations offer a principled bridge: combinatorial objectives can be written as energies, and neural networks can be trained to approximate these energies and sample high-quality solutions. In particular, annealed training has been proposed to cast graph combinatorial optimization problems as unbiased EBMs with carefully selected penalties, yielding more stable unsupervised training and improved solution quality \parencite{sun_annealed_2022}. Progress in discrete sampling benchmarks further highlights the importance of robust, scalable samplers for discrete/structured distributions \parencite{goshvadi_discs_2023}. EBMs have also been explored for non-convex, cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization, illustrating GPU-accelerated sampling as an alternative to single-solution pipelines \parencite{mancilla_non-convex_2026}.

In parallel, differentiable satisfiability and projection layers have been developed to enforce general linear constraints (including those arising in unit commitment), offering end-to-end differentiable mechanisms to project neural outputs back into feasible sets \parencite{zeng_glinsat_2024}. When scenario exploration involves composite and time-varying action spaces (e.g., heterogeneous flexibility levers becoming available/unavailable), structured cooperative RL provides additional design patterns for handling such changing decision sets \parencite{li_structured_2021}. More broadly, generator-enhanced optimization frameworks leverage generative models (classical or quantum-inspired) to improve search-space exploration for hard optimization problems \parencite{alcazar_enhancing_2024}.

\subsection{Research Gap and Research Statement}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Scenario exploration at scale is still solver-bound.} Multi-layer and multi-level MILP formulations remain too costly to support near-real-time exploration when the number of scenarios grows (stress cases, coupled markets, topology changes), despite decomposition and aggregation strategies \parencite{micheli_two-stage_2023,pozo_three-level_2013,xia_scalable_2025,marquant_holarchic_2017}.
  \item \emph{Learning surrogates rarely deliver end-to-end feasibility across layers.} GNN surrogates can be accurate and topology-aware for specific tasks (OPF, risk assessment, restoration), yet guaranteeing feasibility across heterogeneous layers (assets--zones--regions) and across long horizons remains difficult without an explicit feasibility mechanism \parencite{liu_topology-aware_2023,zhang_graph_2024,ahmed_advancing_2024}.
  \item \emph{Limited support for controlled exploration under increasing scenario complexity.} Existing surrogates are often trained for prediction; fewer approaches explicitly address exploration of large discrete decision spaces (commitment logic, discrete DR activation) in a way that remains robust as scenario complexity increases \parencite{yang_solving_nodate,sun_annealed_2022,goshvadi_discs_2023}.
  \item \emph{Weak coupling between economic signals and learned inference.} Multi-level/equilibrium MILP models yield meaningful price signals and strategic interactions, but most learning pipelines do not exploit this structure to steer inference or sampling toward economically consistent solutions \parencite{hobbs_strategic_2000,pozo_three-level_2013,xia_scalable_2025}.
  \item \emph{From point predictions to interactive, near-real-time what-if analysis.} Warm-starting and surrogate prediction accelerate single runs, but the literature provides limited methodology for interactive, user-driven scenario sweeps where the ``complexity knob'' (spatial detail, coupling, uncertainty, topology changes) is increased while preserving speed, feasibility, and interpretability \parencite{deihim_initial_2024,pjetri_graph_nodate,talebi_graph_2025}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{}
The central research question is therefore:
\emph{Can a hybrid MILP--GNN--Latent EBM methodology recast large-scale power-system unit-commitment and dispatch as a compute-bounded stochastic exploration process that delivers high-quality feasibility coverage inside a tight wall-clock budget, and does its relative performance improve as scenario complexity increases (heterogeneous layers, discrete decisions, uncertainty, and topology change)?} The remainder of the paper provides a nuanced answer: the first half of the question is supported throughout the eval set, while the second half is not -- a finding we make explicit in the contributions and in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Contributions}
Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]

  \item \textbf{A compute-bounded benchmark protocol for hybrid solvers on unit commitment.} We define a fixed-budget grid of seven wall-clock budgets $\{1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120\}\,\mathrm{s}$ and a per-scenario matched-budget protocol that gives every solver the same wall-clock as the latent pipeline, with hard-timeout subprocess enforcement so a solver stuck in presolve cannot leak time. We instantiate the protocol on three reference solvers -- the latent pipeline, an unconstrained MILP oracle, and a rolling-horizon merit-order heuristic followed by the same LP cascade (RH-MO+LP) -- and on $300$ stratified evaluation scenarios plus $300$ persona scenarios drawn from three orthogonal stakeholder profiles. The protocol is the apples-to-apples basis on which all subsequent claims are evaluated.

  \item \textbf{A hybrid MILP--GNN--Latent EBM architecture for compute-bounded UC exploration.} The stack pairs a Hierarchical Temporal Encoder over a heterogeneous temporal graph with a UC autoencoder, a JEPA latent predictor that maps each scenario embedding to an anchor in the strategy-latent space, a conditional Latent EBM with cosine-annealed Langevin sampling, and a cascading LP worker that turns each latent candidate into a primal-feasible dispatch plan. Placing the energy function and the Langevin chain in a learned latent space rather than directly on the binary commitment vector keeps the energy gradient stable across topology sizes and is, to our knowledge, the first such design for power-system unit commitment.

  \item \textbf{A hierarchical heterogeneous temporal graph representation of multi-layer flexibility scenarios.} We project each scenario onto a graph that mirrors the asset $\to$ zone $\to$ region $\to$ system containment hierarchy and that carries time as a third dimension. The representation provides a topology-consistent inductive bias for the encoder and for the conditioning of the Latent EBM, and it transfers across the three eval families and the three personas without architectural change.

  \item \textbf{A $22$-axis ex-ante criticality index that decomposes scenario hardness.} The index aggregates twelve stress components $A_1$--$A_{12}$ (renewable penetration, residual volatility, peak-to-valley ratio, inverse storage and thermal flexibility, trade reliance, congestion, etc.) and ten hardness components $B_1$--$B_{10}$ (number of zones, horizon length, estimated binary count, thermal density, ramp tightness, SOC tightness, interconnection density, etc.). It is computable in milliseconds, supports stratified sampling for evaluation, and provides interpretable per-axis features that drive the regime decomposition of C5.

  \item \textbf{An empirical regime decomposition for hybrid solvers.} Through fixed-budget, matched-budget, time-to-first-feasible, driver, compute-bounded, and persona analyses, we identify four operational regimes -- sub-second exploration, mid-budget operational dispatch, hard combinatorial cases, and bulk warm-start generation -- and we map each regime to its preferred solver. The drivers of the matched-budget pipeline advantage are statistically predictable from the criticality components ($70.7\%$ five-fold cross-validated logistic accuracy against a $31\%$ base rate; gradient-boosted $R^2 = 0.41$ for the signed advantage), and the same drivers appear within each family and across all seven budgets, so the regime decomposition is statistically robust rather than budget-specific.

  \item \textbf{A nuanced answer to the original research question, with the negative result identified explicitly.} \emph{Compute-bounded exploration is supported} throughout the eval set: the pipeline is the only solver with non-trivial sub-second feasibility and its pipeline-only-feasible share remains strictly positive on every family at the matched budget. \emph{Scaling with criticality is not supported}: the matched-budget cost gap to MILP-$120\,\mathrm{s}$ grows from $0.23\%$ on low criticality to $8.0\%$ on medium and $129\%$ on high, and the failure mode is structural (the LP cascade aborts at its flip-budget ceiling) rather than statistical. We make this distinction explicit, and we use it to motivate the seven concrete research avenues of Section~\ref{sec:research_avenues} -- including learned candidate selection, learned local search after the LP cascade, MILP warm-start fallback, and criticality-aware routing.

\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:graph_projection} presents the hierarchical heterogeneous temporal graph representation and the topology-consistent encoder that maps each scenario to compact embeddings. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with the data-grounded findings (compute-bounded exploration supported, scaling with criticality not supported), a discussion of the structural failure mode, seven limitations tied to specific evidence, and seven research avenues.


\section{Methodology Overview and Training Data Generation}
\label{sec:methodology}
Figure~\ref{fig:milp-gnn-ebm-pipeline} presents the proposed MILP--GNN--Latent EBM pipeline as an end-to-end exploration engine that transforms high-fidelity optimisation into fast and controllable large-scale scenario sweeps. Starting from a broad pool of synthetic what-if cases, the workflow proceeds as follows: (i) we generate a diversity-maximizing set of scenarios and compute for each of them a scenario-level complexity index capturing combinatorial and operational stress; (ii) a multi-layer MILP oracle is solved to produce optimal solutions; (iii) each instance is projected onto a hierarchical, heterogeneous temporal graph and encoded via a topology-consistent GNN into a compact latent representation; (iv) the unit-commitment binaries are compressed by a small autoencoder (UC-AE) into a low-dimensional \emph{strategy latent}; a UC-JEPA predictor maps the scenario embedding to an anchor in that latent space, and a conditional \emph{latent} energy-based model (EBM) is trained to score candidate strategies and to explore them via Langevin dynamics in the latent space; (v) the lowest-energy latent is decoded back to binaries with per-feature learned thresholds and the resulting candidate is repaired and completed through a feasibility-aware decoder and a conditional LP worker to recover continuous dispatch variables; finally, (vi) the resulting solutions are benchmarked with scenario families of increasing complexity against the pure MILP oracle in terms of runtime, feasibility rate, and cost optimality gap.

\subsection{Scenario Generator}
\label{sec:scenario_generator}

The first stage of our pipeline is a \emph{synthetic scenario generator} that produces a diverse set of power-system flexibility instances to train and evaluate the downstream learning modules. Each scenario encodes a complete specification of the system: network topology, asset portfolio, economic policies, technical constraints, and exogenous drivers (weather, demand, hydro inflows). The generator is designed to (i)~span a wide range of physical stress levels and combinatorial hardness, (ii)~remain computationally tractable for MILP labelling, and (iii)~avoid redundant near-duplicate instances that waste compute budget.

\subsubsection{Scenario Parameter Space}
\label{sec:scenario_space}

A scenario $s$ is fully characterized by a configuration tuple
\begin{equation}
s = \bigl(\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{A}, \pi, \tau, c, \xi\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G}$ is the network graph, $\mathcal{A}$ is the asset specification, $\pi$ denotes economic policy parameters, $\tau$ collects technical scalers, $c$ represents operation costs, and $\xi$ captures exogenous drivers.

\paragraph{Network structure $\mathcal{G}$.}
The spatial hierarchy follows a \emph{assets $\rightarrow$ zones $\rightarrow$ regions $\rightarrow$ nation} structure. For each scenario we sample:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item number of regions $|\mathcal{R}|$,
  \item zones per region $|\mathcal{Z}_r|$ (sampled independently per region),
  \item assets are sampled either at the zone level ($|\mathcal{A}_z$|) or at the region level ($|\mathcal{A}_r$|), depending on the technology (e.g., nuclear units are regional, while solar units are zonal),
  \item intertie density $\rho$ controlling inter-regional transmission,
  \item number of neighbouring nations $|\mathcal{N}|$ for cross-border exchanges.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Asset portfolio $\mathcal{A}$.}
Asset counts are sampled at zone or regional level:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item thermal, run-of-river hydro, solar and wind units per zone,
  \item battery storage and demand-response resources per zone,
  \item nuclear plants, reservoir hydro and pumped-hydro storage per region
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Economic policy $\pi$.}
Policy levers include CO$_2$ price (EUR/t), price cap (EUR/MWh), cross-border policy (free, limited, or blocked), and import/export caps.

\paragraph{Technical scalers $\tau$.}
Key flexibility drivers include thermal ramp rates, minimum generation ratios, battery energy-to-power ratio, round-trip efficiency, final state-of-charge tolerances, and demand-response parameters (max shed share, duration, number of activation events, ramp limits).

\paragraph{Exogenous drivers $\xi$.}
Weather and load conditions are captured by:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item a dominant weather profile (e.g., sunny summer, stormy winter, cloudy spring),
  \item per-region weather cells with profile and spread intensity,
  \item demand profile (weekday peak, weekend flat, etc.) and demand scale factor,
  \item hydro inflow factor.
\end{itemize}
To preserve spatial heterogeneity, each region draws its own weather profile; summary statistics (histogram, entropy, min/mean/max spread) are retained for diversity computation.

\subsubsection{Budget Guard}
\label{sec:budget_guard}

Not all randomly sampled scenarios are tractable within a fixed compute budget. Before acceptance, each candidate $s$ passes through a \emph{budget guard} that estimates MILP size and solve time:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{var}}(s) = f_{\mathrm{var}}(\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{A}, |\mathcal{T}|),
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{T}|$ is the number of time steps. A polynomial time estimator then predicts solve time:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{t}(s) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \widehat{n}_{\mathrm{var}} + \beta_2 \widehat{n}_{\mathrm{cont}} + \beta_3 \widehat{n}_{\mathrm{bin}},
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{var}}$ is the estimated total number of decision variables,
$\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{cont}}$ the estimated number of continuous variables,
and $\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{bin}}$ the estimated number of binary variables
(unit commitment, nuclear commitment, and demand-response activation flags). Scenarios exceeding thresholds on variables, constraints, or predicted solve time are rejected.

\subsubsection{Diversity Maximization}
\label{sec:diversity}

Uniform random sampling tends to cluster in high-dimensional parameter space and over-represents ``easy'' instances. We employ two complementary strategies to maximize useful diversity.

\paragraph{Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS).}
For continuous parameters that drive stress and difficulty (demand scale, inflow factor, CO$_2$ price, intertie density, ramp percentages, storage E/P ratio, import caps), we replace uniform sampling with Latin Hypercube Sampling. LHS partitions each marginal into $N$ equiprobable intervals and ensures exactly one sample per interval, yielding more uniform coverage in high dimensions. Compared to uniform random sampling, LHS reduces clustering and ensures better coverage of extreme parameter combinations.

\paragraph{Greedy $k$-center selection.}
Rather than accepting candidates online, we generate a large candidate pool (e.g., $20 \times$ target size) passing the budget guard, then select the final set via a greedy farthest-point algorithm:
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1} = \arg\max_{s \in \mathcal{P}} \min_{s' \in \mathcal{S}_t} d\bigl(v(s), v(s')\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ is the candidate pool, $\mathcal{S}_t$ is the selected set, and $v(s) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a normalized \emph{diversity vector} encoding topology, asset mix, technical scalers, and exogenous drivers. This ensures a minimum pairwise separation $d_{\min}$ and avoids near-duplicates. Intuitively, this procedure iteratively selects scenarios that are maximally dissimilar from those already selected, thereby spreading the final dataset over the diversity space and avoiding redundant instances.


\paragraph{}
LHS is applied at the parameter-sampling stage to ensure marginal coverage of key continuous drivers, while greedy $k$-center selection operates at the scenario level to enforce global diversity across structural, technical, and exogenous dimensions.

\paragraph{Stratification.}
To prevent over-representation of easy scenarios, we partition candidates into bins along stress and hardness axes (e.g., demand-scale quintiles, topology-size quartiles, weather-profile categories) and allocate quotas per bin before $k$-center selection. Stratification ensures that rare but operationally critical scenarios are sufficiently represented in the training set, rather than being drowned out by a majority of mild instances.


\subsubsection{Scenario Criticality Indicator}
\label{sec:criticality}

To characterize scenario difficulty \emph{ex ante} (before solving), we define a composite criticality indicator decomposed into physical stress and combinatorial hardness:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Crit}(s) = \alpha \, \mathrm{Stress}(s) + (1-\alpha) \, \mathrm{Hard}(s), \quad \alpha \in [0,1].
\end{equation}

All components of $\mathrm{Crit}(s)$ are computable ex ante, without solving the MILP.

\paragraph{Physical Stress Index.}
The stress component captures techno-operational tension through 12 normalized metrics grouped into four categories:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{Variability:} VRE penetration ratio, residual-demand volatility, peak-to-valley ratio, short-term variability index.
  \item \textbf{Load level:} demand scale factor, peak-to-firm capacity ratio.
  \item \textbf{Flexibility margins (inverse):} storage power adequacy, storage energy adequacy, thermal flexibility ratio, DR headroom.
  \item \textbf{Spatial tension:} import/export reliance, congestion exposure proxy.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Combinatorial Hardness Index.}
The hardness component captures structural MILP difficulty through 10 metrics:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{Problem size:} number of zones $|\mathcal{Z}|$, horizon length $|\mathcal{T}|$, estimated binary count.
  \item \textbf{Non-convexity:} thermal unit density, minimum-generation tightness, startup-cost intensity.
  \item \textbf{Temporal coupling:} ramping tightness, SOC tightness proxy.
  \item \textbf{Spatial coupling:} interconnection density, network heterogeneity.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{}
These metrics were selected to jointly capture (i) known drivers of operational stress in power systems and (ii) structural sources of MILP hardness identified in the unit-commitment literature \parencite{buchheim_polytope_2023, huang_cutting_2021, morales_espana_tight_2013}. For completeness and reproducibility, Appendix~\ref{app:criticality} reports the definitions of the 22 metrics used to build the ex ante criticality index.


\paragraph{}
Each metric is normalized to $[0,1]$ using generator-bound scaling and combined as weighted sums:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Stress}(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{12} w_i^{(S)} \tilde{x}_i^{(S)}(s), \quad
\mathrm{Hard}(s) = \sum_{j=1}^{10} w_j^{(H)} \tilde{x}_j^{(H)}(s).
\end{equation}
The criticality index enables stratified sampling and post-hoc analysis of solver performance versus scenario difficulty.

\subsection{Multi-Layer MILP Oracle}
\label{sec:milp_oracle}

The second stage of the pipeline solves, for each generated scenario, a high-fidelity mixed-integer linear program (MILP) that coordinates flexibility resources across the spatial hierarchy defined in Section~\ref{sec:scenario_generator}. The model is a multi-period unit-commitment and economic dispatch formulation that jointly optimizes discrete commitment and activation decisions together with continuous power and storage schedules, subject to network, inter-temporal, and operational constraints. Its solutions provide the ground-truth labels~$(u^*, x^*)$ used to train all downstream learning modules.

Rather than enumerating all modelling variants, we report the oracleâ€™s main constraint families,
with emphasis on discrete decisions and inter-temporal couplings that drive MILP hardness.



\subsubsection{Sets and Indexing}
\label{sec:milp_sets}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $\mathcal{Z}$: set of zones (nested within regions according to the spatial hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:scenario_space}).
  \item $\mathcal{T} = \{0, 1, \dots, T{-}1\}$: set of time steps of uniform duration $\Delta t$ (hours).
  \item $\mathcal{L}$: set of transmission lines connecting zones (intra-regional and inter-regional).
  \item $\mathcal{K} = \{0, 1, \dots, K{-}1\}$: set of demand-response cost tiers (blocks).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Decision Variables}
\label{sec:milp_variables}

The model distinguishes two variable families whose separation is central to the downstream learning pipeline.

\paragraph{Binary (commitment and activation) variables $u$.}
These encode discrete on/off or mode decisions that the EBM sampler will learn to predict:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \in \{0,1\}$: thermal commitment status in zone~$z$ at time~$t$.
  \item $v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \in \{0,1\}$: thermal startup indicator ($v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 1$ if the unit starts up at~$t$).
  \item $\delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} \in \{0,1\}$: demand-response activation flag.
  \item $\sigma^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} \in \{0,1\}$: battery charge/discharge mode ($1$=charge, $0$=discharge). 
  \item $\sigma^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t} \in \{0,1\}$: pumped-hydro charge/discharge mode.
  \item $\sigma^{\mathrm{imp}}_{t} \in \{0,1\}$: cross-border import/export mode ($1$=import).
\end{itemize}

The mode binaries enforce mutual exclusivity while minimizing the number of discrete variables.

\paragraph{Continuous (dispatch and state) variables $x$.}
These are recovered by the LP worker once binaries are fixed:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}, p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: thermal and nuclear power output.
  \item $p^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t}, p^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: dispatched solar and wind power.
  \item $s^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t}, s^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: curtailed (spilled) solar and wind power.
  \item $d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: total demand-response shed; $d^{\mathrm{dr},k}_{z,t} \geq 0$: shed per tier~$k$.
  \item $e^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: demand-response rebound energy backlog.
  \item $q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: unserved energy (load shedding of last resort).
  \item $b^{+}_{z,t}, b^{-}_{z,t} \geq 0$: battery charge and discharge power.
  \item $e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: battery state of charge (SOC).
  \item $h^{\mathrm{rel}}_{z,t}, h^{\mathrm{spl}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: reservoir hydro release and spillage.
  \item $e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: reservoir hydro level.
  \item $g^{+}_{z,t}, g^{-}_{z,t} \geq 0$: pumped-hydro charge and discharge power.
  \item $e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: pumped-hydro storage level.
  \item $f_{\ell,t} \in \mathbb{R}$: power flow on line~$\ell$ (bidirectional).
  \item $m^{+}_{t}, m^{-}_{t} \geq 0$: cross-border import and export power.
  \item $q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t} \geq 0$: over-generation spillage.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
\label{sec:milp_objective}

The objective minimizes total system operating cost over the horizon:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\min_{u,x} \; C^{\mathrm{gen}} + C^{\mathrm{su}} + C^{\mathrm{dr}} + C^{\mathrm{uns}} + C^{\mathrm{spl}} + C^{\mathrm{sto}} + C^{\mathrm{imp}} + C^{\mathrm{ovg}},
\end{equation}
where each component aggregates over all zones and time steps:
\begin{align}
C^{\mathrm{gen}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} \bigl( c^{\mathrm{th}}_z \, p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} + c^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z \, p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t} \bigr), \label{eq:cost_gen} \\
C^{\mathrm{su}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} c^{\mathrm{su}}_z \, v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}, \label{eq:cost_startup} \\
C^{\mathrm{dr}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} c^{\mathrm{dr}}_k \, d^{\mathrm{dr},k}_{z,t}, \label{eq:cost_dr} \\
C^{\mathrm{uns}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} c^{\mathrm{voll}} \, q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t}, \label{eq:cost_uns} \\
C^{\mathrm{spl}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} \bigl( c^{\mathrm{res}} (s^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t} + s^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t}) + c^{\mathrm{hsp}} \, h^{\mathrm{spl}}_{z,t} \bigr), \label{eq:cost_spill} \\
C^{\mathrm{sto}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} \bigl( c^{\mathrm{cyc,bat}}_z (b^{+}_{z,t} + b^{-}_{z,t}) + c^{\mathrm{cyc,phs}}_z (g^{+}_{z,t} + g^{-}_{z,t}) \bigr), \label{eq:cost_storage} \\
C^{\mathrm{imp}} &= \textstyle\sum_{t} \bigl( c^{\mathrm{imp}} \, m^{+}_{t} + c^{\mathrm{exp}} \, m^{-}_{t} \bigr), \label{eq:cost_trade} \\
C^{\mathrm{ovg}} &= \textstyle\sum_{z,t} c^{\mathrm{ovg}} \, q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t}. \label{eq:cost_overgen}
\end{align}

The thermal fuel cost~$c^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ includes a CO$_2$ component ($c^{\mathrm{th}}_z = c^{\mathrm{fuel}} + c^{\mathrm{marg}} + \pi_{\mathrm{CO}_2} \cdot \varepsilon$), and the demand-response tier costs~$c^{\mathrm{dr}}_k$ increase exponentially across blocks ($c^{\mathrm{dr}}_k = c^{\mathrm{dr}}_0 \cdot 1.5^{k}$) to model increasing activation reluctance. The value of lost load $c^{\mathrm{voll}}$ acts as a large penalty ensuring load shedding is a last resort.

\subsubsection{Constraint Families}
\label{sec:milp_constraints}

The model enforces nine families of constraints whose full mathematical formulation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:milp_constraints}. We summarize them here to convey the sources of MILP hardness.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\roman*)}, noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Thermal unit commitment.}
    Output is bounded between minimum stable generation and rated capacity, scaled by the commitment binary~$u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}$. Symmetric ramp-up/down limits couple consecutive periods, and a startup indicator $v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}$ detects $0{\to}1$ commitment transitions to incur startup costs.

  \item \textbf{Nuclear baseload.}
    Nuclear units are always online (must-run) but may modulate output within capacity and ramp limits ($R^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z = 0.5\,\overline{P}^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z$), reflecting limited short-term flexibility while allowing moderate intra-day adjustment.

  \item \textbf{Renewable curtailment.}
    Solar and wind generation equal available power minus a non-negative curtailment variable, penalized in the objective.

  \item \textbf{Demand response.}
    DR is modelled with $K$ cost tiers of increasing marginal cost, big-M activation constraints linking the binary~$\delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}$ to continuous shedding, a maximum number of activation events per zone, a rebound energy backlog with exponential decay that must clear by the end of the horizon, and inter-temporal ramp limits.

  \item \textbf{Battery storage.}
    The mode binary~$\sigma^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t}$ enforces mutual exclusivity of charge and discharge. State-of-charge evolution accounts for asymmetric round-trip efficiencies and self-discharge, with end-of-horizon SOC bounded within a tolerance band around the initial level.

  \item \textbf{Reservoir hydro.}
    Reservoir level evolves with natural inflows minus release and spillage, both bounded by capacity.

  \item \textbf{Pumped-hydro storage.}
    Mirrors the battery formulation with its own mode binary, round-trip efficiencies, self-discharge retention, and final level bounds.

  \item \textbf{Transmission and cross-border exchanges.}
    Line flows are bounded by capacity (bidirectional). Cross-border import and export are mutually exclusive via the mode binary~$\sigma^{\mathrm{imp}}_t$ and bounded by aggregate interconnection capacity.

  \item \textbf{Nodal power balance.}
    At every zone and time step, total generation plus demand response, unserved energy, and net inflows must equal demand plus storage charging, outflows, and over-generation spillage. Cross-border exchanges enter the balance at a single designated anchor zone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Variable Fixing and Solver Strategy}
\label{sec:milp_fixing}

To tighten the formulation and reduce solve time, the model pre-fixes binary variables for inactive assets: thermal commitment is fixed to zero in zones with no thermal capacity, DR activation flags are fixed where DR capacity is zero, and storage mode variables are fixed for zones without the corresponding storage asset. Similarly, the import mode binary is fixed when no cross-border capacity exists. This pre-processing eliminates unnecessary branching and typically reduces the effective binary count by 30--60\%, depending on scenario sparsity.

Each scenario is solved with a configurable time limit ($\overline{T}_{\mathrm{solve}}$). Instances that terminate within the time limit yield optimal primal solutions, are labeled \emph{gold labels} and will be used for pretraining of the EBM; those that time out are classified as \emph{silver labels} and represent the best incumbent feasible found within the time limit. These incumbents will only be used for further training of the EBM.

\subsubsection{LP Relaxation and Dual Information}
\label{sec:milp_duals}

As part of the oracle's data-generation pass, the same unit-commitment model is also solved a second time with all binary variables relaxed to their continuous $[0,1]$ counterparts. This LP relaxation serves two purposes: (i)~it provides a lower bound on the optimal cost (the integrality gap is stored alongside each scenario), and (ii)~it extracts dual variables (shadow prices) for key constraints---in particular, the nodal power-balance duals yield locational marginal prices, and the transmission-capacity and storage-level duals reveal congestion rents and storage shadow values.

Note that this LP relaxation is distinct from the conditional LP worker used later in the pipeline (Section~5) to recover continuous dispatch after the EBM sampler has predicted binary decisions.


\subsubsection{Model Size and Complexity}
\label{sec:milp_size}
These structural propertiesâ€”binary explosion, strong inter-temporal coupling, and tight end-of-horizon constraintsâ€”are known to severely limit decomposition and to challenge branch-and-bound methods, especially under high stress scenarios.
Indeed, the number of decision variables and constraints scales with the product of zones, time steps, and asset types. For a scenario with $|\mathcal{Z}|$ zones, $|\mathcal{T}|$ time periods, $|\mathcal{L}|$ lines, and $K$ DR tiers, the approximate model dimensions are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Binary variables:} $\sim 4\,|\mathcal{Z}|\,|\mathcal{T}| + |\mathcal{T}|$ (thermal commitment and startup, DR activation, battery and pumped-hydro modes, plus import mode), before pre-fixing.
  \item \textbf{Continuous variables:} $\sim (14 + K)\,|\mathcal{Z}|\,|\mathcal{T}| + |\mathcal{L}|\,|\mathcal{T}| + 2\,|\mathcal{T}|$.
  \item \textbf{Constraints:} $\sim (16 + 2K)\,|\mathcal{Z}|\,|\mathcal{T}| + 2\,|\mathcal{L}|\,|\mathcal{T}| + 4\,|\mathcal{Z}| + 2\,|\mathcal{T}|$.
\end{itemize}
In our benchmark suite (Table~\ref{tab:scenario-comparison}), this ranges from ${\sim}\,45\,000$ variables and ${\sim}\,58\,000$ constraints for simple scenarios to ${\sim}\,139\,000$ variables and ${\sim}\,181\,000$ constraints for critical ones, confirming that repeated solves across thousands of scenarios quickly become the computational bottleneck that motivates the downstream learning pipeline.

\section{Representation learning}
\label{sec:graph_encoder}

A key design choice of the proposed pipeline is to \emph{not} flatten each scenario into a fixed-length feature vector. Doing so would destroy the spatial topology, discard the variable-size structure (the number of zones, regions, and assets changes from one scenario to the next), and ignore the relational constraints that are the very source of MILP complexity. Instead, we project every scenario onto a \emph{multi-layer heterogeneous temporal graph} and learn an encoder that produces compact embeddings while respecting the containment hierarchy and temporal couplings.

This section gives the motivation and high-level design of the two components: graph construction (Section~\ref{sec:graph_projection}) and the Hierarchical Temporal Encoder (Section~\ref{sec:hte}). Formal definitions, complete edge-type catalogues, and architectural equations are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:graph_hte_details}.

\subsection{Projection onto Multi-Layer Heterogeneous Temporal Graphs}
\label{sec:graph_projection}

\subsubsection{Why a graph?}

Energy system scenarios are naturally hierarchical: a single nation contains several regions, each region groups multiple load zones, and each zone owns a portfolio of generation and storage assets. These relationships---together with the transmission network that connects zones and the weather patterns that drive renewable output---are most faithfully captured by a graph whose structure mirrors the physical system. A graph representation offers three advantages over tabular alternatives:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Variable size.} The number of zones, assets, and inter-ties varies across scenarios. A graph accommodates this naturally, whereas a flat vector requires padding or truncation that introduces artefacts.
  \item \textbf{Explicit topology.} Containment (nation $\to$ region $\to$ zone $\to$ asset), transmission lines, and weather influence are encoded as typed edges, giving the downstream encoder direct access to the relational structure that governs MILP feasibility.
  \item \textbf{Temporal coupling.} By replicating the spatial graph at every time step and adding inter-temporal edges (state-of-charge continuity, ramping limits, demand-response cooldowns), the graph encodes the same coupling constraints that make the MILP difficult to solve.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Graph structure at a glance}

Each scenario is mapped to a heterogeneous attributed graph $\mathcal{G}(s)$ with five node types and eleven edge types (seven spatial, four temporal). The construction is fully determined by the scenario specification and requires no MILP solution, so the same builder can be used at inference time for unseen scenarios.

\paragraph{Nodes.}
The graph contains one \emph{nation} node (system-wide aggregates), one \emph{region} node per geographical region, one \emph{zone} node per load zone, one \emph{asset} node per active technology instance, and one \emph{weather} node per region. Asset nodes are attached to the appropriate spatial level: zonal technologies (thermal, solar, wind, battery, run-of-river hydro, demand response) are children of their parent zone, while large-scale resources defined at the regional level (nuclear, reservoir hydro, pumped hydro) are children of their parent region. An asset node is only created when the corresponding capacity is strictly positive, so the graph size varies across scenarios.

\paragraph{Spatial edges.}
Containment edges form a strict tree (nation $\to$ regions $\to$ zones $\to$ zonal assets, plus region $\to$ regional assets). Weather-influence edges connect each weather cell to the zones and renewable assets in its region. Bidirectional transmission edges link zones that share a power line, carrying the line's thermal capacity and electrical distance as attributes.

\paragraph{Temporal extension (supra-graph).}
To capture inter-temporal MILP constraints, each base node is replicated at every time step~$t \in \{0,\dots,T{-}1\}$, and directed temporal edges are added between consecutive copies of the same asset: \emph{SOC edges} for storage assets, \emph{ramp edges} for thermal and nuclear generators, and \emph{cooldown edges} for demand-response units. The result is a \emph{supra-graph} whose size scales as $N_{\mathrm{base}} \times T$. Sinusoidal positional encodings are concatenated to node features to provide periodicity-aware time information.

The complete schema---node feature dimensions, all eleven edge types, and temporal edge construction rules---is given in Appendix~\ref{app:graph_schema}. We provide bellow two illustrative figures of the spatial (Figure~\ref{fig:graph_spatial})  and temporal (Figure~\ref{fig:graph_temporal}) graph structures to help picture what a our graph representation look like.

\subsection{Hierarchical Temporal Encoder}
\label{sec:hte}

\subsubsection{Why not a standard graph neural network?}

The supra-graph constructed above can contain a very large number of nodes. Applying a standard dense-attention graph Transformer to such inputs would require $\mathcal{O}(N^2 T^2)$ memory---prohibitive even on modern GPUs. At the same time, a shallow message-passing network that treats all nodes uniformly would fail to distinguish between local asset-level interactions (e.g., a battery's SOC evolution within a single zone) and global system-level patterns (e.g., the national demand--supply balance over a day). The encoder must therefore be \emph{hierarchy-aware}: it should aggregate local information upward, reason about temporal dynamics at the system level, and then distribute this global context back to the fine-grained nodes that need it.

\subsubsection{Three-phase architecture}

We introduce a \emph{Hierarchical Temporal Encoder} (HTE) that factorises computation into three phases, mirroring the natural information flow in power system operations:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Bottom-up spatial encoding.} Information flows upward through the containment hierarchy: asset-level representations are aggregated into zones, zones into regions, and regions into a single nation-level summary. At each level, graph attention layers~\citep{brody_how_2022} allow neighbouring nodes to exchange information (e.g., assets sharing a zone, zones connected by transmission lines) before pooling. Skip connections preserve local detail at every level for later use.

  \item \textbf{Temporal Transformer at the nation level.} Once the entire system state has been compressed into a single node per time step, a standard Transformer encoder~\citep{vaswani2017attention} captures temporal dependencies across the horizon. Because the sequence length equals the number of time steps ($T = 24$ in the benchmark), dense self-attention is computationally negligible, yet it has access to the full spatial context at each step.

  \item \textbf{Top-down decoding.} The temporally enriched nation representation is broadcast back through the hierarchy: first to regions, then to zones, then to assets. At each level, the broadcast signal is combined with the skip connection saved during the bottom-up phase, so that the final embeddings fuse global temporal context with local spatial detail.
\end{enumerate}

The output is a set of temporal embedding vectors at every hierarchical level---assets, zones, regions, and nation---each of dimension $D = 128$. The zone-level embeddings are the primary conditioning signal consumed by the downstream EBM (Section~4), while the asset-level embeddings drive the self-supervised training objective.


\subsubsection{Self-supervised training}
\label{sec:hte_training}

A distinguishing feature of the HTE is that it is trained \emph{without any MILP solution labels}. The training signal comes entirely from the structure of the scenario graphs themselves, through a contrastive learning objective.

\paragraph{Intuition.}
The key idea is that the embedding of a given asset at time~$t$ should be similar to the embedding of \emph{the same asset a few time steps later} (a ``positive pair''), and dissimilar to embeddings of \emph{unrelated assets at random times} (``negative pairs''). This encourages the encoder to learn representations that capture the temporal dynamics of the system---ramping patterns, charge/discharge cycles, demand response activations---without needing to see any optimal dispatch solution.

\paragraph{Multi-lag design.}
Rather than using a single lag (e.g., $t \to t{+}1$), we form positive pairs at three lags: $\tau \in \{1, 4, 8\}$ hours. This ensures the encoder captures dynamics at multiple time scales: short-range (hourly ramping and SOC transitions), medium-range (intra-day patterns), and longer-range (diurnal cycles). The loss is the InfoNCE objective~\citep{oord2018representation}, a standard contrastive loss widely used in self-supervised representation learning. Because training uses the asset level---the finest granularity---the loss implicitly shapes all coarser levels through the pooling and skip-connection pathways.

\paragraph{Training setup.}
The model is trained on 4\,000 scenario graphs (with 1\,000 additional scenarios held out for validation) using the AdamW optimiser~\citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with cosine learning-rate annealing and early stopping. Because graph sizes vary widely ($N_{\mathrm{base}} \in [33, 851]$), only one graph fits in GPU memory at a time; gradient accumulation over 8~steps provides an effective batch size of~8. On a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, training converges in ${\sim}\,80$ epochs (${\sim}\,8$~hours); generating embeddings for all $5\,000$ scenarios takes an additional hour.

The complete architectural equations, layer-by-layer specifications, and the full hyperparameter table are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:hte_details}, along with an architecture diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:hte_architecture}).

\section{Energy-Based Learning in a UC Strategy Latent}
\label{sec:ebm}

The encoder of Section~\ref{sec:graph_encoder} compresses each scenario into per-(zone, time) embeddings $h \in \mathbb{R}^{Z \times T \times D}$. These embeddings capture \emph{what the system looks like}---topology, asset portfolio, weather, demand---but they say nothing about \emph{what to do}. We bridge this gap with a three-module \emph{latent UC stack}: (i) a \emph{UC autoencoder} (UC-AE) compresses the binary commitment tensor $u^\star \in \{0,1\}^{Z \times T \times F}$ of Section~\ref{sec:milp_variables} into a small bag of continuous \emph{strategy tokens} $z_{uc} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}$; (ii) a \emph{UC-JEPA} predictor maps the scenario embedding $h$ to an anchor $z_{\mathrm{anc}}$ in that same latent space; (iii) a \emph{latent EBM} $E_\phi(z \mid z_{\mathrm{anc}}, h)$ scores candidate strategies, which are explored by Langevin dynamics in $\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$ and decoded back to per-feature thresholded binaries.

This factorisation has two practical benefits over an EBM defined directly on $\{0,1\}^{Z\times T \times F}$. First, the AE absorbs the structural regularities of MILP-optimal schedules (start-up sequences, charge/discharge cycles, DR activations), so that the EBM only has to model a low-dimensional manifold of \emph{strategies} rather than a high-dimensional binary tensor. Second, Langevin dynamics in the AE latent space is genuinely continuous, sidesteps any logit-space relaxation, and its per-step cost depends on $K, d, D$ but not on $Z$ or $T$, so sampling scales gracefully with scenario size.

\subsection{Decision representation}
\label{sec:ebm_features}

Each MILP solution is projected onto a binary tensor $u \in \{0,1\}^{Z \times T \times F}$ with $F=8$ features per (zone, time):
\begin{equation}
u_{z,t} = \bigl[\,
  \underbrace{b^{\mathrm{ch}}_{z,t},\, b^{\mathrm{dis}}_{z,t}}_{\text{battery}},\;
  \underbrace{g^{\mathrm{ch}}_{z,t},\, g^{\mathrm{dis}}_{z,t}}_{\text{pumped}},\;
  \underbrace{\delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}}_{\text{DR}},\;
  \underbrace{v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t},\, u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}}_{\text{thermal}},\;
  \underbrace{\iota^{\mathrm{imp}}_{z,t}}_{\text{imports}}
\,\bigr],
\label{eq:ebm_features}
\end{equation}
where the first seven entries are the storage modes, DR activation, thermal start-up and commitment status of Section~\ref{sec:milp_variables}, and $\iota^{\mathrm{imp}}_{z,t} = \mathbb{1}[\text{net imports}_{z,t} > 0]$ is a per-zone import-mode indicator added to expose cross-border flexibility to the latent stack. Variable zone counts are handled with a binary mask $m \in \{0,1\}^{B \times Z_{\max}}$ that excludes padded zones from every loss and pooling operation.

\subsection{UC autoencoder}
\label{sec:uc_ae}

The UC-AE compresses $u^\star$ into a fixed bag of $K$ \emph{strategy tokens}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{UCEnc}: \{0,1\}^{Z\times T\times F} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}, \qquad
\mathrm{UCDec}: \mathbb{R}^{K\times d} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{Z\times T\times F}.
\label{eq:ae_signature}
\end{equation}
The encoder reads zone trajectories with a BiGRU over $T$, mean-pools in time, and lets $K$ learned query tokens cross-attend over the (masked) zones to produce $z_{uc} = \mathrm{UCEnc}(u^\star)$. The decoder builds learned (zone, time) queries that cross-attend over $z_{uc}$ and emits per-(zone, time) logits, with the option of adding an MLP projection of $h$ as a per-(zone, time) bias to give the decoder explicit access to the scenario. Detailed architecture and equations are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:uc_ae}.

The AE is trained on gold MILP labels by minimising
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AE}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}\bigl(u^\star,\, \mathrm{UCDec}(z_{uc})\bigr) + \lambda_t\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}} + \lambda_c\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{su}},
\label{eq:ae_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}$ is the masked binary cross-entropy with per-feature positive weights to counter the strong class imbalance (e.g.\ DR and start-ups are rare events), $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}}$ penalises $|p_{z,t+1} - p_{z,t}|$ on the decoded probabilities to encourage temporal smoothness, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{su}}$ enforces the soft start-up consistency $v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \approx \mathrm{ReLU}(u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} - u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1})$. Once trained, the AE is frozen and used both as the target for the JEPA predictor and as the decoder for samples produced by the latent EBM.

\subsection{UC-JEPA scenario-to-latent predictor}
\label{sec:uc_jepa}

The JEPA predictor maps the scenario embedding $h$ to an anchor in the AE latent space:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{JEPA}: \mathbb{R}^{Z \times T \times D} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}, \qquad
z_{\mathrm{anc}} = \mathrm{JEPA}(h, m).
\label{eq:jepa_signature}
\end{equation}
A shared BiGRU produces per-(zone, time) hidden states; a zone-summary attention head emits the latent prediction, while optional per-(zone, time) auxiliary heads decode the storage modes, DR, thermal commitment / start-up, and a system-level import flag directly from $h$. Architecture details are in Appendix~\ref{app:uc_jepa_arch}.

The predictor is trained against the \emph{frozen} AE encoder, $z^\star_{uc} = \mathrm{UCEnc}(u^\star)$, by combining a JEPA-style latent regression with anti-collapse terms and end-to-end UC supervision through the frozen AE decoder:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JEPA}}
   &= w_m\, \big\| z_{\mathrm{anc}} - \mathrm{sg}(z^\star_{uc} + \epsilon)\big\|_2^2
   + w_v\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}(z_{\mathrm{anc}})
   + w_c\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cov}}(z_{\mathrm{anc}}) \notag \\
   &\quad + w_u\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}\!\bigl(u^\star,\, \mathrm{UCDec}(z_{\mathrm{anc}})\bigr)
   + w_a\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(u^\star, h),
\label{eq:jepa_loss}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient, $\epsilon$ a small target noise, and the variance / covariance regularisers
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}(z) = \mathrm{mean}_d\,[\gamma - \mathrm{std}(z)_d]_+, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cov}}(z) = \tfrac{1}{D}\!\sum_{i \neq j}\,\mathrm{Cov}(z)_{ij}^{\,2},
\label{eq:vc_reg}
\end{equation}
prevent the trivial constant-mean solution that pure MSE on a frozen target tends to converge to (the standard JEPA failure mode). The decoded BCE term and the auxiliary heads inject direct decision-level supervision so that the predicted latent stays useful after decoding rather than simply matching the AE marginal distribution. Per-feature positive weights (and an optional focal exponent) re-balance the rare DR / start-up / import classes; full details and head-level equations are in Appendix~\ref{app:uc_jepa_arch}.

\subsection{Latent EBM}
\label{sec:latent_ebm}

The latent EBM scores candidate strategy latents conditioned on the JEPA anchor and a pooled scenario context:
\begin{equation}
E_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{K\times d} \times \mathbb{R}^{K\times d} \times \mathbb{R}^{D} \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}, \qquad
E_\phi\!\bigl(z \mid z_{\mathrm{anc}}, \bar h\bigr),
\label{eq:lebm_signature}
\end{equation}
where $\bar h \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the masked mean of $h$ over (zone, time). Per-token features stack $[z,\, z_{\mathrm{anc}},\, z - z_{\mathrm{anc}},\, z \odot z_{\mathrm{anc}}]$ and pass through a token-wise MLP whose outputs are mean- and max-pooled over the $K$ tokens; this is concatenated with first/second-order statistics of $z_{\mathrm{anc}}$, the pooled context $\bar h$, the squared norms $\|z\|_2^2$ and $\|z - z_{\mathrm{anc}}\|_2^2$, and the cosine similarity $\cos(z, z_{\mathrm{anc}})$, before a final scalar head. The raw output is bounded by $E_{\max} \tanh(\cdot / E_{\max})$ to stabilise training. The full architectural specification is given in Appendix~\ref{app:latent_ebm_arch}.

The EBM is trained on top of the frozen AE and JEPA with a contrastive-divergence objective. Positives come from the AE encoder, negatives from short latent-Langevin chains initialised near the anchor:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LEBM}} = \mathbb{E}\bigl[E_\phi(z^\star_{uc} \mid z_{\mathrm{anc}}, \bar h)\bigr] - \mathbb{E}\bigl[E_\phi(z^- \mid z_{\mathrm{anc}}, \bar h)\bigr] + \alpha_{\mathrm{reg}}\,R(E_\phi),
\label{eq:lebm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $R(E_\phi)$ softly penalises out-of-range energies and $z^-$ is obtained by the latent Langevin sampler of Section~\ref{sec:latent_langevin}. Because the AE and JEPA are frozen, $E_\phi$ is a small, lightweight head that adds only a fraction of the joint parameter count.

\subsection{Latent Langevin sampling}
\label{sec:latent_langevin}

At inference, a continuous Langevin chain in $\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$ explores low-energy strategies around the JEPA anchor:
\begin{equation}
z^{(k+1)} = z^{(k)} - \eta_k\,\hat g^{(k)} - \eta_k\,\lambda_d\,\bigl(z^{(k)} - z_{\mathrm{anc}}\bigr) + \sqrt{2\eta_k\,T_k}\,\sigma_k\,\varepsilon^{(k)}, \quad \varepsilon^{(k)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
\label{eq:latent_langevin}
\end{equation}
with $\hat g^{(k)} = \nabla_z E_\phi / \|\nabla_z E_\phi\|$ a unit-norm gradient (gradient normalisation keeps the chain stable across scenarios of very different scales), $\lambda_d$ an anchor-drift coefficient that prevents the chain from leaving the JEPA neighbourhood, and $(\eta_k, T_k, \sigma_k)$ a cosine-annealed step / temperature / noise schedule. The chain is initialised either by perturbing $z_{\mathrm{anc}}$ or by drawing from an empirical latent prior $\mathcal{N}(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$ estimated once on the training set, with the two modes mixed by a tunable probability. Latents are clipped to a fixed box to prevent numerical drift. Among $N$ parallel chains, the lowest-energy latent is retained:
\begin{equation}
\hat z = \arg\min_{n \in [N]}\, E_\phi\!\bigl(z^{(K), n} \,\big|\, z_{\mathrm{anc}}, \bar h\bigr).
\label{eq:best_of_n}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decoding to binaries with learned per-feature thresholds}
\label{sec:learned_thresholds}

The selected latent $\hat z$ is pushed through the frozen AE decoder to per-(zone, time, feature) probabilities $\hat p_{z,t,f} = \sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{UCDec}(\hat z)_{z,t,f}\bigr)$. Because the eight UC features have very different positive-class rates (e.g.\ thermal commitment is active most of the time, DR activation and start-ups are sparse), a single global threshold of $0.5$ is severely suboptimal. We therefore learn a per-feature threshold vector $\boldsymbol\tau \in [0,1]^F$ once on a held-out validation split:
\begin{equation}
\tau_f \;=\; \arg\max_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}}\; F_1\!\Bigl(\mathbb{1}[\hat p_{\cdot,\cdot,f} \geq \tau],\; u^\star_{\cdot,\cdot,f};\; m\Bigr),
\qquad
\mathcal{T} = \{0.20, 0.25, \dots, 0.80\},
\label{eq:learned_threshold}
\end{equation}
with $F_1$ the masked feature-wise $F_1$ score. The vector $\boldsymbol\tau$ is persisted with the EBM checkpoint and reused at inference: $\hat u_{z,t,f} = \mathbb{1}[\hat p_{z,t,f} \geq \tau_f]$. This calibration step is what makes the rare but operationally critical features (DR, thermal start-ups, imports) usable downstream; it cannot be replaced by reweighting the BCE loss alone, because the optimal operating point depends on the joint distribution induced by the AE / JEPA rather than on each marginal independently.

\subsection{Three-stage training}
\label{sec:ebm_training}

Training proceeds in three sequential, monotonic stages so that each module sees a stable upstream signal:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Stage A --- UC-AE on gold labels.} The AE is trained to reconstruct $u^\star$ on the gold split of MILP labels (Eq.~\ref{eq:ae_loss}); the encoder is then frozen for all later stages.
  \item \textbf{Stage B --- UC-JEPA on $(h, u^\star)$.} The predictor is trained against the frozen AE encoder and decoder via Eq.~\ref{eq:jepa_loss}, using gold and silver labels jointly. Per-feature thresholds $\boldsymbol\tau$ are tuned on the JEPA validation split (Eq.~\ref{eq:learned_threshold}) and stored alongside the predictor.
  \item \textbf{Stage C --- Latent EBM.} With AE and JEPA frozen, the latent EBM is trained on gold and silver labels by contrastive divergence in latent space (Eq.~\ref{eq:lebm_loss}); the latent Langevin chain (Eq.~\ref{eq:latent_langevin}) produces the negative samples used in the loss.
\end{enumerate}
Decoupling the three stages avoids the joint-training pathologies (mode collapse, AE drift, energy-scale runaway) that we observed when training the modules end-to-end, and keeps each loss directly interpretable. The complete hyperparameter configuration, including class-imbalance weights, the focal exponent used for rare events, and the curriculum on the Langevin step count, is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:latent_stack_hp}.

\subsection{From samples to feasible plans}
\label{sec:ebm_inference}

At inference, a new scenario is processed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Encode.} Build the heterogeneous temporal graph and run the HTE to obtain $h \in \mathbb{R}^{Z\times T\times D}$.
  \item \textbf{Anchor.} Compute $z_{\mathrm{anc}} = \mathrm{JEPA}(h, m)$.
  \item \textbf{Explore.} Run $N$ parallel latent Langevin chains (Eq.~\ref{eq:latent_langevin}) on the EBM $E_\phi$ and keep the lowest-energy latent $\hat z$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:best_of_n}).
  \item \textbf{Decode.} Push $\hat z$ through the frozen AE decoder and apply the learned thresholds $\boldsymbol\tau$ to obtain $\hat u \in \{0,1\}^{Z\times T\times F}$.
  \item \textbf{Warm-start and dispatch.} The pass-through warm-start decoder builds a physically consistent continuous warm-start $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ for $\hat u$ (Section~\ref{sec:feasibility_decoder}); the cascading LP worker (Section~\ref{sec:lp_worker}) then recovers the continuous dispatch $\hat x$, relaxing a small set of binaries only if Stage~1 leaves residual slack.
  \item \textbf{Select.} If multiple $(\hat u, \mathrm{cost})$ pairs are evaluated, keep the one with the lowest LP cost.
\end{enumerate}
Steps~1--4 are a sequence of differentiable forward passes whose cost scales with $K, d$ and $D$ but not with the binary tensor size $Z\times T\times F$; step~5 is identical to the LP-based reconstruction used for any other commitment skeleton. The pipeline therefore replaces the combinatorial search of branch-and-bound with $N_s$ parallel decode-and-solve passes ($N_s{=}5$ in our experiments, so reported pipeline times include $5\times$ the decoder and LP cost), while preserving the auditability of the LP stage.

\section{Solution Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The latent UC stack of Section~\ref{sec:ebm} produces binary decision candidates $\hat{u} \in \{0,1\}^{Z \times T \times F}$ ($F{=}8$) that encode commitment and mode schedules for every zone and time step. However, these candidates are \emph{not} directly usable as power-system dispatch plans: they say nothing about the continuous variables (power levels, storage trajectories, cross-border flows), and even a perfectly commitment-consistent schedule still leaves the LP solver to reconcile demand, storage dynamics, and ramping. The present section describes the two components that bridge this gap: a \emph{pass-through warm-start decoder} that turns $\hat{u}$ into a physically consistent continuous warm-start without touching any binary, and a \emph{cascading LP worker} that solves the original UC model with the binaries fixed and selectively relaxes only the smallest set of binaries needed when the hard-fixed problem proves too tight.

Together, these components form a \emph{reconstruct-then-optimise} pipeline: the warm-start decoder provides a fast, deterministic physical projection (milliseconds), while the LP worker performs an exact optimisation of the continuous sub-problem. This decomposition exploits the classical insight that, once the discrete decisions are fixed, the remaining unit-commitment problem reduces to a linear program that can be solved efficiently and to global optimality.


\subsection{Pass-through Warm-start Decoder}
\label{sec:feasibility_decoder}

In the latent inference pipeline the role of the feasibility decoder is deliberately \emph{narrow}: it preserves the binaries $\hat{u}$ produced by the latent UC stack and constructs a continuous dispatch $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ that is consistent with those binaries and with the scenario physics (capacities, demand, weather, storage parameters, ramp limits, SOC bounds, efficiencies). The pair $(\hat{u}, x_{\mathrm{ws}})$ is then handed to the LP worker, where $\hat{u}$ is hard-fixed and $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ is loaded as the solver's initial primal solution to crash the LP basis.

\subsubsection{Why pass-through rather than repair?}

Earlier prototypes used the same component as a binary \emph{repair} heuristic: when $\hat{u}$ proved infeasible at some $(z, t)$, the decoder flipped a small number of bits via a merit-order fallback. Two reasons drove the change to a pass-through design for the experiments reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Auditability.} A pass-through decoder makes the contribution of the latent UC stack directly observable downstream: every binary that reaches the LP solver is exactly a binary produced by the JEPA / latent EBM, so any binary repair is the responsibility of the LP worker, not a hidden heuristic.
  \item \textbf{Better LP starts.} The LP worker can always relax some binaries on its own (Section~\ref{sec:lp_worker}), but it cannot easily discover a physically consistent continuous starting point. A deterministic warm-start that already respects ramp, SOC, and efficiency constraints is therefore worth more than a binary correction, and avoids two consecutive layers of heuristic flipping.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Three-phase forward dispatch}

The decoder processes one time step at a time, tracking storage state-of-charge and thermal dispatch history across the horizon. At each time step it executes three phases that mirror the MILP constraint families (Appendix~\ref{app:milp_constraints}); crucially, no binary in $\hat{u}$ is ever overruled---if $\hat u$ flags a resource OFF, the decoder leaves the corresponding continuous variable at zero and lets the resulting imbalance show up as unserved energy or overgeneration in $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$.

\paragraph{Phase~1: Must-run and free resources.}
Nuclear baseload and available renewables (solar, wind, run-of-river hydro) are dispatched first, as they have zero or near-zero marginal cost. The residual demand after this phase determines whether the system is locally in deficit or surplus.

\paragraph{Phase~2: Deficit handling, binaries-respecting.}
If the residual is positive, the decoder allocates capacity in the following priority and \emph{only} to resources whose binary in $\hat{u}$ is set:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Nuclear headroom (remaining capacity beyond the must-run minimum).
  \item Reservoir hydro (if water is available).
  \item Pumped-hydro then battery discharge, gated by $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{p,dis}}_{z,t}$ and $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{b,dis}}_{z,t}$.
  \item Thermal generation, gated by $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}$ and clamped by ramp limits and minimum stable generation.
  \item Cross-border imports.
  \item Demand response, gated by $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}$ and subject to a maximum activation duration over the horizon.
  \item Unserved energy absorbs whatever residual is left.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Phase~3: Surplus handling, binaries-respecting.}
If the residual is negative, excess generation is absorbed in the following priority:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Cross-border exports (restricted to the anchor zone, matching the MILP model).
  \item Thermal reduction (respecting ramp-down limits and minimum stable generation).
  \item Renewable curtailment (wind first, then solar).
  \item Pumped-hydro then battery charging, gated by $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{p,ch}}_{z,t}$ and $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{b,ch}}_{z,t}$.
  \item Overgeneration spillage (last resort).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{MILP-consistent physics}

The forward dispatch mirrors the constraint families of the full MILP (Appendix~\ref{app:milp_constraints}) so that $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ falls inside the LP-feasible region whenever $\hat{u}$ does:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Storage dynamics.} Separate charge and discharge efficiencies ($\eta^+$, $\eta^-$) and per-period self-discharge retention ($\rho$), matching the MILP's SOC evolution equation~\eqref{eq:battery_soc}.
  \item \textbf{Thermal ramp limits.} Dispatch at time~$t$ is clamped to $[p_{t-1} - R, \, p_{t-1} + R]$, with a startup exception that allows jumping directly to $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}$ from zero whenever $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1} = 0$ and $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 1$.
  \item \textbf{Anchor-zone exports.} Only the designated anchor zone may export, while imports are distributed across all zones as a proxy for network flows.
  \item \textbf{Thermal minimum generation.} If a thermal unit is committed, its dispatch is forced to at least $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}$ to avoid the infeasible region between 0 and $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}$.
\end{itemize}

The full algorithmic specification, including storage SOC tracking, DR duration accounting, and the zone-by-zone forward loop, is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:reconstruction_details}.


\subsection{Cascading LP Worker}
\label{sec:lp_worker}

The pass-through decoder yields a physically consistent warm-start, but the binaries themselves are still the latent stack's predictions and may occasionally be too tight for the MILP constraint set (e.g., a thermal commitment pattern that breaks startup--shutdown coupling, or a storage schedule that overshoots the final SOC tolerance). The LP worker therefore applies a short, three-stage cascade that solves the original UC model with all binaries hard-fixed first, then relaxes an increasing but still small subset of binaries when residual slack remains.

\subsubsection{Cascade rationale}

Fixing all binaries and solving the resulting LP is fast and sufficient for the vast majority of scenarios. When it is not, we want to relax \emph{as few binaries as possible}: each unfixed binary turns the problem from an LP back into a small MILP, and an unbounded relaxation would dissolve the speed advantage of the pipeline. The cascade therefore expands the set of editable binaries in a controlled way, escalating only when the previous stage failed to drive total slack (unserved + overgeneration) below a tolerance $\epsilon$.

\subsubsection{Stage progression}

The LP worker proceeds through up to three stages, stopping as soon as the total slack is below $\epsilon$:

\paragraph{Stage~1: Hard-fix LP.}
All binary variables (thermal commitment, storage modes, DR activation) are fixed to $\hat{u}$. The remaining integer variables (e.g., import/export mode) are relaxed to continuous, yielding a pure LP that inherits the warm-start $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ from Section~\ref{sec:feasibility_decoder}. If the total slack is below the tolerance, the solution is accepted immediately.

\paragraph{Stage~2: Local repair.}
If Stage~1 leaves excessive slack, the LP worker scores every $(z, t)$ pair by slack magnitude and thermal tension (capacity mismatch between the fixed commitment and the optimal dispatch, see Appendix~\ref{app:reconstruction_details}) and unfixes the $K=20$ highest-scoring pairs across all binary families (thermal, storage, DR), with a temporal $\pm 1$ window to respect inter-temporal coupling. The resulting small MILP is solved with a soft deviation penalty $\lambda$ on the released binaries and a flip-budget constraint $B_2$ that caps the total number of bit flips. This stage is intentionally local: it can only touch a few dozen $(z, t)$ pairs.

\paragraph{Stage~3: Wider repair.}
If Stage~2 still leaves residual slack, the same construction is repeated with $K = 100$ critical pairs and a larger flip budget $B_3$, allowing more substantial corrections at the price of a longer time limit.

If Stage~3 also fails to clear the slack tolerance within its time budget, the scenario is reported as a pipeline failure and excluded from the cost-quality analysis. This is the only outcome of the cascade other than a feasible $(\hat u, \hat x)$ pair, and it occurs in a measurable fraction of the high-criticality family (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsubsection{Deviation penalty and flip budget}

Two mechanisms keep the LP worker's repairs close to the latent UC stack's intent:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Deviation penalty.} For each unfixed binary $u_{z,t}$, auxiliary variables $d^+_{z,t}, d^-_{z,t} \geq 0$ satisfy $u_{z,t} - \hat{u}_{z,t} = d^+_{z,t} - d^-_{z,t}$, and the objective is augmented with $\lambda (d^+ + d^-)$. The high penalty discourages flips except where strictly necessary for feasibility.
  \item \textbf{Flip budget.} A hard constraint $\sum_{z,t} (d^+_{z,t} + d^-_{z,t}) \leq B$ limits the total number of binary flips per stage, ensuring localised corrections rather than wholesale rewrites of the commitment schedule.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{End-to-End Reconstruction Cost}
\label{sec:reconstruction_cost}

The combined warm-start decoder plus LP worker is designed for throughput. The decoder runs in pure Python/PyTorch with no external solver calls, completing in a few tens of milliseconds per scenario. The LP worker's Stage~1 (the common case) solves a continuous LP with the same constraint structure as the original MILP but no branch-and-bound. Even when Stages~2--3 are triggered, the total reconstruction time remains well below the MILP solve time (which can reach minutes to hours for hard scenarios), because the LP worker never re-solves the full combinatorial problem---it only edits a small set of binaries around the latent stack's suggestion, with explicit caps on both the number of unfixed indices and the number of allowed flips.

This asymmetry is the key design insight: the \emph{hard} combinatorial decisions are handled by the fast latent UC stack and pass-through warm-start, while the \emph{easy} continuous optimisation, together with a small targeted repair when needed, is delegated to a mature LP / small-MILP solver. The result is a reconstruction path that combines the speed of learned inference with the exactness of mathematical programming.

% 6.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOL
% ============================================================
\subsection{Evaluation protocol under bounded compute}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

The pipeline is given a fixed total wall-clock budget of $T_{\max} = 120\,\mathrm{s}$ per scenario; the scenario\-to\-binary part of the stack (graph build, embedding, JEPA, latent EBM, latent Langevin, decoder) runs once, and the remaining budget is split equally across the $K$ candidate strategies feeding into the LP cascade (Section~\ref{sec:lp_worker}). Unless stated otherwise we report \emph{$K=1$} -- a single-shot pipeline -- and we revisit $K=5$ in Section~\ref{sec:compute_bounded}.

We compare the three solvers under two complementary protocols.

\subsubsection{Grid-time-constrained MILP (baseline 1)}
\label{sec:protocol_milp}
The MILP oracle is re-solved from scratch on every scenario at each fixed wall-clock budget in $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120\}\,\mathrm{s}$ (seven points, geometrically spaced). Each call sets HiGHS' \texttt{time\_limit} to the corresponding budget and is wrapped in a subprocess with a hard \texttt{kill()} a few seconds past the budget so a solver stuck in presolve cannot leak time. A run is counted as feasible only if the subprocess returns a primal-feasible incumbent strictly within its budget. In addition to the seven fixed budgets, we record one \emph{matched-budget} solve per scenario at exactly the pipeline's measured wall-clock time. This matched-budget protocol is the apples-to-apples comparison: each solver gets the same per-scenario time as the pipeline did on that very scenario.

\subsubsection{Grid-time-constrained RH-MO+LP (baseline 2)}
\label{sec:protocol_heuristic}
The RH-MO+LP heuristic of Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} re-uses the cascading LP worker but replaces the latent UC stack with a deterministic rolling-horizon merit-order schedule: at every horizon step it activates the cheapest dispatchable mix that satisfies a one-step-ahead net-demand projection, then commits the resulting binaries to a six-step look-ahead before sliding the window forward. The same grid $\mathcal{B}$ and the same per-scenario matched budget are applied; the heuristic and its LP cascade run together inside a subprocess with a hard \texttt{kill()}. Because the binary schedule is produced in milliseconds, the bulk of the heuristic's wall-clock is spent in the cascading LP worker exactly like the pipeline, which lets us isolate the value of \emph{learned} binaries over a hand-crafted rule.

Three patterns are visible. \subsubsection{Compute-bounded exploration: testing the $K$-scaling hypothesis}
\label{sec:compute_bounded}

The compute-bounded exploration hypothesis states that, at fixed total wall-clock $T_{\max}$, drawing $K{>}1$ Langevin candidates and keeping the best one improves the pipeline's solution quality. We test it by re-running the pipeline at $K=5$ on the same $300$ scenarios under the same $120\,\mathrm{s}$ budget. The per-candidate budget therefore drops from $120\,\mathrm{s}$ to $\sim 24\,\mathrm{s}$, while the latent UC stack is amortised once across the $K$ candidates.

Figure~\ref{fig:compute_bounded} reports two complementary views. The left panel is the best-of-$K$ feasibility curve: for each scenario in the $K=5$ run we expose the cost of every candidate and keep the running best. The right panel is the paired delta on the $K=1$ vs $K=5$ runs, restricted to scenarios where both runs returned a feasible incumbent (the only cases where a paired Wilcoxon test is well-defined).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_compute_bounded_k_scaling.png}
  \caption{Compute-bounded exploration: $K=5$ versus $K=1$ at the same $120\,\mathrm{s}$ total wall-clock. Left panel: best-of-$K$ feasibility curve from the $K=5$ run, by family. Feasibility climbs monotonically with $K$ on medium ($86 \to 97\%$) and high ($33 \to 52\%$); low is already saturated at $K=1$. Right panel: paired distribution of $(\mathrm{cost}_{K=5} - \mathrm{cost}_{K=1}) / |\mathrm{cost}_{K=1}|$ on scenarios where both runs are feasible (boxplots, by family).}
  \label{fig:compute_bounded}
\end{figure}

The hypothesis is supported on the \emph{feasibility} dimension: increasing $K$ from $1$ to $5$ raises the share of scenarios on which at least one candidate clears the LP cascade, by $4$ percentage points on medium ($86 \to 97\%$) and by $19$ points on high ($33 \to 52\%$, almost a doubling). The cost dimension is more nuanced. Within paired feasible scenarios the median delta is $0.0\%$ on low (already saturated), $-0.23\%$ on medium (slight improvement), and $+1.7\%$ on high (small sample, $n=12$); none of the three Wilcoxon $p$-values reach $0.05$. The interpretation is that, at the same total $120\,\mathrm{s}$, $K{=}5$ converts compute into \emph{additional feasible coverage} rather than into significantly cheaper plans. This is consistent with the LP-driven nature of the cascade: once a candidate clears Stage 1, its objective is already very close to the MILP-LP relaxation lower bound, so further candidates compete on a tight floor.

A second, less obvious effect is that the per-candidate budget shrinks from $120\,\mathrm{s}$ to $\sim 24\,\mathrm{s}$ when $K=5$, which truncates the LP cascade's deepest stages. On the high-criticality family this trade-off costs $6$ percentage points of \emph{selected} feasibility ($24 \to 18\%$ when comparing the singled-out best-of-energy candidates) even though the best-of-$K$ feasibility envelope grows. Selecting the right candidate from the $K$-bag is therefore a separate problem -- a learned candidate selector trained on these traces is the natural follow-up.

\label{sec:criticality_scaling}

Figure~\ref{fig:criticality_scaling} reports the matched-budget joint outcome (pipeline-only feasible / both feasible / MILP-only feasible) by family, on the eval set of $N=300$ scenarios.

The two halves of the original research question therefore receive different verdicts. \emph{Compute-bounded exploration} (does the pipeline find feasible plans inside a tight budget where MILP cannot?) is supported throughout the eval set: even on high criticality the pipeline alone is feasible on $19\%$ of scenarios where MILP cannot meet the matched budget. \emph{Scaling with criticality} (does the relative pipeline advantage \emph{grow} as scenarios get harder?) is not supported: as criticality grows, the pipeline's coverage advantage shrinks and its cost gap to the MILP optimum widens.

\subsection{Persona-driven evaluation}
\label{sec:personas}

The eval families of Section~\ref{sec:eval_scenarios} are sampled uniformly across the criticality space; they leave open the question of how the pipeline behaves on \emph{user-shaped} scenario families that emphasise specific drivers. We therefore generate three additional families of $100$ scenarios each, parameterised by the persona configurations of the scenario generator: the \emph{VRE-and-battery} persona (storage-heavy, very high VRE penetration), the \emph{network operator} persona (large topology, high zone count, tight ramping), and the \emph{mathematician} persona (adversarially specified, designed to maximise binary problem size).

The matched-budget protocol therefore reinforces the same conclusion as the eval families: the latent pipeline is the only solver that converts a small wall-clock budget into reliable feasibility coverage across personas of different criticality profiles.

\subsubsection{Persona drivers of pipeline advantage and failure}
\label{sec:persona_drivers}

Two persona-level mechanisms dominate the picture. First, on the \emph{VRE-and-battery} persona the latent UC stack benefits from a marginal scenario distribution that is very close to its training set: the JEPA anchor lands in a low-energy region of the latent EBM and a single Langevin chain already returns a near-feasible binary plan, which the pass-through decoder + LP cascade then certify. This is the regime where the compute-bounded exploration argument is strongest: the pipeline returns a feasible warm-start in seconds while the MILP oracle is still in presolve.

Second, the failure mode on the \emph{mathematician} persona is the mirror image of the high-criticality eval result of Section~\ref{sec:criticality_scaling}: the binary count is high enough that the LP cascade's flip budgets ($B_2 = 100$, $B_3 = 1000$) are no longer sufficient to repair a moderately-good but imperfect latent prediction, and the cascade aborts at Stage~3. The fix is structural rather than statistical: either (i) increase $B_3$ at the cost of LP solve time, (ii) replace Stage~3 with a learned local search head, or (iii) feed the latent candidate as a MILP warm-start rather than as a hard-fixed binary, letting branch-and-bound repair the residual infeasibilities. The first is straightforward; the second and third are research avenues we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:research_avenues}.

The \emph{network-operator} persona is more nuanced. Three observations summarise it. (i) At the $120\,\mathrm{s}$ fixed budget RH-MO+LP attains the highest feasibility ($77\%$) of the three solvers, because the persona's large but ramp-flexible topology lets a merit-order schedule + LP polish converge to \emph{some} primal-feasible plan more often than a single MILP solve. (ii) That plan, however, carries a $47\%$ median cost gap to the MILP-120\,s reference, so RH-MO+LP is dominated on the cost dimension by both the pipeline ($21\%$ gap) and the unconstrained MILP. (iii) At the matched per-scenario budget ($\sim 35\,\mathrm{s}$), the pipeline retains $66\%$ feasibility against $18\%$ for MILP -- the pipeline's compute-bounded-exploration advantage is concentrated on exactly the same drivers identified in Table~\ref{tab:drivers}, namely loose interconnection density and moderate ramping. The persona evaluation therefore confirms, on a different sampling distribution, the same picture as the eval families: the pipeline is strongest in the regime where its training distribution is dense and where the LP cascade can still repair small binary errors quickly; the rolling-horizon heuristic provides a useful upper feasibility envelope where the topology is large but the unit-commitment combinatorics remain merit-order-friendly; and the MILP oracle becomes the only reliable solver as soon as the binary problem hits the cascade's flip-budget ceiling.

\subsection{Partial-grid recovery under contingencies}
\label{sec:contingency_recovery}

The evaluation so far scores a commitment by its base-case feasibility and dispatch cost. An operationally harder question is whether a commitment stays \emph{dispatchable} once the network is degraded by line outages. This subsection reports a preliminary single-contingency ($N{-}1$) study: given the unit-commitment produced by each solver, can the continuous dispatch be re-optimised to survive every single-line outage, and at what load-shed cost?

\paragraph{Protocol.} We draw a $30$-scenario pilot ($10$ per criticality family) from the eval set of Section~\ref{sec:eval_scenarios}. For each scenario we freeze a solver's \emph{thermal commitment} and solve a security-constrained economic redispatch (SCED) -- an approximate DC power-flow LP with line susceptances proportional to distance, soft thermal limits, and load-shedding as the last resort -- for the base case and the $12$ most critical single-line outages ($13$ dispatch solves per commitment); storage, demand response and cross-border exchange stay re-dispatchable. We compare four commitment sources: \textbf{A}, the full MILP oracle; \textbf{B}, a partial MILP that optimises only a critical zone subset $S^\star$ ($40\%$ of zones, peak/firm rule) and fills the remainder with an all-thermal-on heuristic; \textbf{C}, the learned pipeline (full); and \textbf{D}, the pipeline restricted to $S^\star$ with the same heuristic fill. A contingency is \emph{recovered} iff the redispatch is feasible \emph{and} sheds at most $\tau = 1\,\mathrm{MWh}$; a scenario is \emph{fully recovered} iff all $13$ cases are recovered. The high family is much slower, so $4$ of its $10$ scenarios exceeded the per-scenario wall-clock budget and were dropped ($n=6$ there); the high numbers are therefore indicative rather than definitive.

\paragraph{Feasibility is governed by global co-optimisation, not by learning.} Only the full MILP (A) keeps a dispatch-feasible commitment across every family (Table~\ref{tab:contingency}, Figure~\ref{fig:contingency_feasibility}). Every commitment that is \emph{not} globally co-optimised -- the all-thermal-on heuristic fill (B) and both learned variants (C, D) -- degrades identically: $100\%$ feasible on low criticality, $80\%$ on medium, $33\%$ on high. Crucially, B fails on exactly the same scenarios as C and D, which identifies the mechanism as \emph{over-commitment}: pinning too many thermal units online forces minimum-generation overgeneration that load-shedding cannot absorb, so the redispatch is infeasible even in the base case. The deficiency is therefore about co-optimising the on/off pattern across the whole grid, not a defect of the learned model in particular.

\paragraph{A feasibility-versus-robustness tension.} The cost-optimal MILP commitment is always feasible but carries little reserve, so it sheds load under outages and is fully recovered on only $10\%$ of medium scenarios. The reserve-heavy partial and learned commitments recover more often \emph{where they survive} -- arm D reaches $40\%$ full recovery on medium against $10\%$ for A -- because the extra committed capacity provides headroom. That same extra commitment is what renders them outright infeasible on the hardest scenarios. Lean optimal commitments are thus always dispatchable but fragile to shed; conservative commitments are more robust where feasible but risk collapse on the tightest cases.

\paragraph{At the hard end, guaranteed feasibility wins.} On a per-contingency basis the ranking flips with criticality: arm A recovers a mean fraction $0.40$ of high-criticality outages versus $\sim 0.33$ for B--D, precisely because A never collapses to infeasibility. This mirrors the Section~\ref{sec:criticality_scaling} result that the MILP oracle is the only reliable solver at the hard end. We stress that the load-shed magnitudes in Figure~\ref{fig:contingency_recovery} (right) are conditioned on feasibility and thus survivorship-biased; the feasibility and full-recovery rates are the trustworthy headline. A full $N{-}1$ study on all $300$ scenarios and a security-constrained training signal are the natural next steps (Section~\ref{sec:research_avenues}, R6).

\section{Conclusions and Research Avenues}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

This paper introduced a hybrid \textbf{MILP--GNN--Latent EBM} methodology for compute-bounded exploration of multi-scale power-system flexibility scenarios. The stack combines six tightly integrated components: a synthetic scenario generator with diversity maximisation and an ex-ante criticality index $\mathcal{C}$ (Section~\ref{sec:scenario_generator}); a multi-layer MILP oracle coordinating thermal commitment, storage scheduling, demand response, and cross-border exchanges (Section~\ref{sec:milp_oracle}); a projection of each scenario onto a heterogeneous temporal graph that mirrors the physical containment hierarchy (Section~\ref{sec:graph_projection}); a Hierarchical Temporal Encoder (HTE) trained by self-supervised contrastive learning (Section~\ref{sec:hte}); a JEPA latent predictor followed by a conditional latent Energy-Based Model with cosine-annealed Langevin sampling (Section~\ref{sec:ebm}); and a cascading LP worker that turns latent candidates into certified primal-feasible dispatch plans (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}).

We evaluated this stack against two reference solvers under matched protocols (Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocol}): an unconstrained MILP oracle and a rolling-horizon merit-order heuristic followed by the same LP cascade (RH-MO+LP). The evaluation spans $300$ out-of-sample scenarios stratified by criticality (Section~\ref{sec:eval_scenarios}) and $300$ persona scenarios drawn from three contrasting stakeholder profiles (Section~\ref{sec:personas}). Each solver is given the same wall-clock budget at seven fixed grid points $\{1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120\}\,\mathrm{s}$ and at the pipeline's per-scenario matched budget, with hard-timeout subprocess enforcement. We additionally probe commitment robustness with a $30$-scenario single-contingency ($N{-}1$) redispatch pilot (Section~\ref{sec:contingency_recovery}).


\subsection{Key findings}
\label{sec:key_findings}

\paragraph{Compute-bounded exploration is supported.}
The pipeline is the only solver with non-trivial feasibility at sub-second budgets: at $1\,\mathrm{s}$ it already returns feasible plans on $33\%$ of low-criticality scenarios while both MILP and RH-MO+LP are blocked by their respective fixed costs (HiGHS presolve and $\sim 7\,\mathrm{s}$ subprocess startup). At the per-scenario matched budget, the pipeline-only-feasible share remains strictly positive across every family ($41\%$ low / $33\%$ medium / $19\%$ high; Section~\ref{sec:criticality_scaling}). This is the first half of our original research question, and it is supported throughout the eval set: the pipeline expands the feasibility envelope reachable inside a tight wall-clock.

\paragraph{Scaling with criticality is not supported.}
The pipeline's relative advantage shrinks as the criticality index grows. Pipeline-only feasibility falls $41 \to 33 \to 19\%$ across the low / medium / high families, the both-feasible share collapses $58 \to 26 \to 1\%$, and the MILP-only-feasible share explodes $1 \to 29 \to 49\%$. The matched-budget median absolute cost gap to MILP-120\,s grows from $0.23\%$ on low to $8.0\%$ on medium and $129\%$ on high (Figure~\ref{fig:matched_budget_3way}). The pipeline is therefore a fast-exploration engine, not an automatic replacement for the MILP oracle at the hard end of the spectrum.

\paragraph{Where the pipeline wins is statistically predictable.}
A driver analysis on the criticality components (Section~\ref{sec:driver_analysis}) attributes the matched-budget pipeline advantage to loose ramping ($B_7$), abundant thermal flexibility ($A_9$ low), moderate peak-to-valley ratios ($A_3$), and small total demand ($A_5$). A logistic classifier of ``pipeline wins'' on these features reaches $70.7\%$ five-fold cross-validated accuracy against a $31\%$ base rate, and a gradient-boosted regressor of the signed advantage attains a five-fold $R^2 = 0.41$. The same drivers appear within each family and across all seven budgets (Figure~\ref{fig:driver_heatmap}), so the regime decomposition of Table~\ref{tab:regimes} is statistically robust rather than budget-specific.

\paragraph{Compute-bounded $K$-scaling buys coverage, not cheaper plans.}
Running the pipeline with $K=5$ Langevin candidates at the same $120\,\mathrm{s}$ total budget raises the best-of-$K$ feasibility envelope by $19$ percentage points on high criticality ($33 \to 52\%$) and $11$ points on medium ($86 \to 97\%$). The paired Wilcoxon test on scenarios where both $K=1$ and $K=5$ are feasible does not reject the null in any family ($p > 0.3$); median cost deltas are $-0.23\%$ (medium) and $+1.7\%$ (high, $n=12$). At fixed total wall-clock, additional Langevin candidates therefore diversify feasibility rather than refine cost; selecting the right candidate from the bag is a separate, currently unsolved problem.

\paragraph{RH-MO+LP only competes in one corner.}
The hand-crafted heuristic is the most feasible solver on the network-operator persona at $120\,\mathrm{s}$ ($77\%$ vs $66\%$ pipeline and $56\%$ MILP) and reaches $99\%$ feasibility on the low-criticality family at the same budget. Outside this regime it is dominated, either by the pipeline's sub-second feasibility (which RH-MO+LP cannot match because of its subprocess startup) or by MILP's optimality (RH-MO+LP carries a $47\%$ median cost gap on network-operator and on high criticality, against essentially zero for MILP).

\paragraph{Persona evaluation reproduces the regime structure.}
The three personas span almost orthogonal regions of the $22$-axis criticality decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:persona_radar}). The pipeline dominates the VRE-and-battery persona ($73\%$ feasibility at $120\,\mathrm{s}$ vs $27\%$ MILP), is competitive on the network-operator persona ($66\%$ vs $56\%$ MILP), and loses to MILP on the adversarial mathematician persona ($27\%$ vs $72\%$). Persona results therefore reproduce, on a different sampling distribution, the same qualitative regime structure as the eval families (Section~\ref{sec:persona_main}).


\paragraph{Commitment robustness under contingencies is a co-optimisation property.}
A preliminary $N{-}1$ study (Section~\ref{sec:contingency_recovery}) freezes each solver's thermal commitment and re-optimises the dispatch under single-line outages. Only the fully co-optimised MILP commitment stays dispatch-feasible across all criticality bands; the all-thermal-on heuristic fill and both learned commitments remain feasible on every low-criticality scenario but on only $33\%$ of high-criticality ones, failing on identical scenarios by over-committing thermal units beyond what load-shedding can absorb. Where the learned commitments \emph{are} feasible they carry more reserve and are fully recovered more often than the lean cost-optimal MILP plan (e.g.\ $40\%$ vs $10\%$ on medium), exposing a feasibility-versus-robustness tension. The finding reinforces the routing view: contingency robustness, like raw feasibility at the hard end, is best guaranteed by the exact oracle.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The combined evidence motivates a four-regime deployment chart (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}): sub-second exploration is the pipeline's natural home; mid-budget operational dispatch is a tie between pipeline and MILP and should be selected by the drivers of Table~\ref{tab:drivers}; hard combinatorial cases belong to the MILP oracle; and bulk warm-start generation is again pipeline territory because the best-of-$K$ feasibility envelope at $K=5$ exceeds what either reference solver delivers in the same total budget.

Two qualitative observations complete the picture. First, the pipeline's per-scenario wall-clock is dominated by the LP cascade -- the latent components (graph construction, HTE, JEPA, EBM, Langevin, decoder) run in a few hundred milliseconds even on the largest topologies -- so the same architecture would benefit asymmetrically from a faster LP solver or from warm-started LP relaxations. Second, the cost-gap distribution is heavy-tailed: when the cascade does return a plan on high criticality, that plan is often very far from the MILP optimum (median $129\%$ gap). This suggests that the failure mode is structural -- the cascade aborts before reaching its deepest stages -- rather than statistical, and motivates the structural research avenues below.


\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We identify seven limitations of the current framework. Each is tied to concrete evidence in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} and to a research avenue in Section~\ref{sec:research_avenues}.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{L\arabic*}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{The pipeline's value does not yet scale with hardness.} Section~\ref{sec:criticality_scaling} shows that pipeline-only feasibility, both-feasible coverage, and pipeline cost quality all degrade monotonically with the criticality index $\mathcal{C}$. The MILP oracle remains the only reliable solver on the hard end ($\mathcal{C} \ge 0.55$).

  \item \textbf{Best-of-$K$ selection is unsolved.} $K=5$ improves the feasibility envelope (Section~\ref{sec:compute_bounded}) but the singled-out best-of-energy candidate is sometimes worse than the $K=1$ result -- selected feasibility on high criticality drops from $24\%$ ($K=1$) to $18\%$ ($K=5$) even as the best-of-$K$ envelope reaches $52\%$. The Langevin energy is not yet a reliable cost selector.

  \item \textbf{LP cascade flip budgets are a structural ceiling.} On the mathematician persona and on the eval high family, the cascade aborts at Stage~3 when the predicted binary plan is too far from feasibility ($B_2 = 100$, $B_3 = 1000$ flip-budget caps; Section~\ref{sec:persona_drivers}). The fix is structural: more flip budget, a learned local-search head, or a MILP warm-start fallback -- none of which is yet in the stack.

  \item \textbf{Single-horizon, deterministic, security-uncoupled setting.} The MILP oracle and the entire pipeline operate on a single $24$-hour deterministic horizon and optimise commitments \emph{without} contingency constraints. The $N{-}1$ study of Section~\ref{sec:contingency_recovery} is a \emph{post-hoc} redispatch on a $30$-scenario pilot (with $4$ hard high-criticality scenarios dropped on the per-scenario wall-clock budget), not a security-constrained commitment. Multi-day or multi-week rolling horizons, stochastic scenarios with correlated forecast errors, and security-constrained training would substantially increase the binary decision space and are out of scope of the current evaluation.

  \item \textbf{Persona RH-MO+LP only ran on the fixed-budget grid.} The persona heuristic was evaluated on the seven grid budgets only; its feasibility at the per-scenario matched budget is reported as the smallest-grid-budget upper bound (Figure~\ref{fig:persona_matched}), not as an exact value. An exact matched-budget RH-MO+LP run on the personas would tighten the Section~\ref{sec:persona_main} numbers.

  \item \textbf{Training-distribution dependency.} All learned components were trained on scenarios from a single synthetic generator. The within-family and within-persona consistency of the driver rankings (Section~\ref{sec:driver_analysis}) suggests that the pipeline's advantage transfers across the three eval families, but cross-generator transfer is not validated; the persona evaluation is the closest available proxy.

  \item \textbf{No validation on real-world operational data.} The $300$ evaluation scenarios are synthetic. Validation on data from a real transmission-system operator is necessary before any operational deployment; such data is rarely public at the required granularity.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Research avenues}
\label{sec:research_avenues}

The limitations above point to seven concrete directions, ordered by how directly they convert the current evidence into a stronger stack.

\paragraph{R1: Learned candidate selector (addresses L2).}
The compute-bounded $K$-scaling experiment already produces LP-certified objectives for every candidate in every scenario. Training a lightweight selector whose target is the LP-certified objective (rather than the Langevin energy) would close the gap between the best-of-$K$ feasibility envelope and the singled-out best-of-energy candidate. No additional data collection is required: the existing $K=5$ traces are sufficient.

\paragraph{R2: Learned local search after the LP cascade (addresses L1, L3).}
When Stage~3 aborts, the partial binary plan can be handed off to a learned local-search head that performs a fixed budget of commitment swaps guided by the LP shadow prices, before re-solving the LP. The shadow prices already identify the most violated constraints; the head only has to predict which binary flips remove them. This directly addresses the heavy-tailed failure mode on high criticality and on the mathematician persona.

\paragraph{R3: MILP warm-start fallback (addresses L1, L3).}
Rather than hard-fixing the best-of-$K$ candidate's binaries, feed it as a MILP warm-start and let branch-and-bound repair residual infeasibilities under the same wall-clock budget. This converts the pipeline's persistent sub-second feasibility advantage into a strict improvement over a cold MILP solve in the regimes where the cascade alone cannot certify the candidate.

\paragraph{R4: Stochastic and multi-period extensions (addresses L4).}
Replace single-trajectory scenarios with scenario trees, turn the MILP oracle into a two-stage or multi-stage stochastic program, condition the latent EBM on an embedding distribution rather than a point embedding, and extend the Langevin sampler to the joint here-and-now / wait-and-see decision space. Rolling-horizon deployment then requires the encoder and EBM to handle overlapping time windows with state carry-over.

\paragraph{R5: Criticality-aware routing (addresses L1).}
The criticality index $\mathcal{C}$ and the driver features of Table~\ref{tab:drivers} are computable in milliseconds. A production router can use them to dispatch each incoming scenario to the pipeline, the MILP oracle, or RH-MO+LP, with a self-tuning threshold updated online from observed solve outcomes. The regime table of Table~\ref{tab:regimes} is the static version of this rule; the dynamic version is essentially a contextual bandit over solvers.

\paragraph{R6: Security-constrained extension (addresses L1, L4).}
The preliminary $N{-}1$ study of Section~\ref{sec:contingency_recovery} shows that contingency feasibility is governed by \emph{global} commitment co-optimisation, and that lean cost-optimal commitments trade shed-robustness for guaranteed feasibility. The avenue is to fold $N{-}1$ contingency constraints into the MILP oracle's labels, re-train the encoder, EBM and cascade on contingency-robust commitments, and extend the criticality index with a contingency-severity component so the routing rule of R5 can treat security as an explicit axis. Scaling the pilot to all $300$ scenarios with a hard per-contingency feasibility metric is the immediate first step.

\paragraph{R7: Transfer across system configurations (addresses L6, L7).}
Test whether the HTE embeddings and the latent EBM transfer to scenarios from different countries, asset mixes, and market designs with minimal fine-tuning, ultimately ending on real transmission-system-operator data. The graph-based representation and the self-supervised JEPA training provide a favourable inductive bias, but cross-generator and real-data validation remains to be done.


\subsection{Closing remarks}

The hybrid MILP--GNN--Latent EBM stack is best understood as a compute-bounded exploration engine. Its role is to convert a small wall-clock budget into reliable feasibility coverage over a large catalogue of scenarios; the exact MILP solver remains the right tool when the budget is large or when the scenario is at the hard end of the criticality spectrum. The most valuable downstream use of the pipeline is therefore not to replace the MILP oracle, but to feed it: best-of-$K$ candidates from the latent stack are warm-starts that an exact solver can certify, and the criticality index is the routing signal that tells the production system which solver to invoke. The seven research avenues above trace a path from the current evaluation to a production-grade exploration engine for the multi-scale flexibility challenges of the energy transition.

\section{Criticality index: metric definitions}
\label{app:criticality}

\subsection{Normalization and aggregation}
Each raw metric $x_k(s)$ is mapped to $[0,1]$ through bounded min--max scaling:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_k(s)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{x_k(s)-\ell_k}{u_k-\ell_k},\,0,\,1\right),
\label{eq:crit_norm}
\end{equation}
where $(\ell_k,u_k)$ are metric-specific bounds (static, or data-driven percentile bounds).
The stress and hardness indices are computed as weighted sums of normalized metrics:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Stress}(s)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_S} w_k^{(S)}\,\tilde{x}_k(s),\qquad
\mathrm{Hard}(s)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_H} w_k^{(H)}\,\tilde{x}_k(s),
\end{equation}
and the overall criticality index is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Crit}(s)=\alpha\,\mathrm{Stress}(s)+(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{Hard}(s),\qquad \alpha\in[0,1].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Metric definitions}
\label{app:crit_metrics}

We list below the 22 raw metrics used in the implementation. For readability we define the common proxy constants used in the code and reuse them across metrics.

\paragraph{Common proxy constants}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item Average unit sizes: $\overline{P}_{\mathrm{th}}=300$ MW (thermal), $\overline{P}_{\mathrm{nuc}}=1000$ MW (nuclear).
\item Average zonal demand: $\overline{D}_Z=500$ MW.
\item Peak multiplier: $\kappa_{\mathrm{peak}}=1.3$.
\item Capacity factor proxy for variable-cost scaling: $\kappa=0.5$.
\item Variable cost proxy: $c_{\mathrm{var}}=60$ EUR/MWh.
\item Startup cost proxies: $c^{\mathrm{SU}}_{\mathrm{th}}=5000$ EUR, $c^{\mathrm{SU}}_{\mathrm{nuc}}=30000$ EUR.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Auxiliary quantities.}
Let $n_Z$ be the number of zones, $n_{\mathrm{th}}$ and $n_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ the number of thermal and nuclear units, $H$ the horizon in hours, and $\lambda_D$ the demand scale factor.
We use the firm-capacity and peak-demand proxies:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{firm}}(s)=n_{\mathrm{th}}\overline{P}_{\mathrm{th}}+n_{\mathrm{nuc}}\overline{P}_{\mathrm{nuc}},
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{peak}}(s)=n_Z\,\overline{D}_Z\,\lambda_D\,\kappa_{\mathrm{peak}}.
\label{eq:aux_peak_firm}
\end{equation}
We also define a residual-peak proxy (used in A7--A9):
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{res,peak}}(s)=D_{\mathrm{peak}}(s)\Bigl(1-0.3\,x_{A1}(s)\Bigr).
\label{eq:aux_respeak}
\end{equation}
The clipping operator used in several metrics is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{clip}(y,a,b)=\max\{a,\min\{y,b\}\}.
\label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}

% ============================================================
% A) Physical stress metrics (A1--A12)
% ============================================================

\subsubsection*{A. Physical stress metrics (A1--A12)}

\paragraph{A1: VRE penetration ratio.}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{A1}(s)=\frac{p^{\mathrm{VRE}}}{100}.
\end{equation}
Here $p^{\mathrm{VRE}}$ is the VRE penetration in percent.

\paragraph{}High VRE penetration displaces dispatchable generation and introduces forecast-dependent supply variability. Criticality emerges because the residual demand (demand minus VRE infeed) becomes more volatile and less predictable, forcing the unit-commitment solver to explore more binary on/off combinations for thermal and storage assets to maintain power balance across all timesteps.

\paragraph{A2: Residual demand volatility (proxy).}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{A2}(s)=\sigma_{\mathrm{res}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\mathrm{res}}$ is a scenario-level proxy of net/residual demand volatility.

\paragraph{}Residual demand volatility measures how much the net load (demand minus must-run renewables) fluctuates over the horizon. High volatility requires frequent changes in thermal commitment and storage mode, multiplying the number of binary switching decisions the solver must evaluate. It is also a primary driver of ramping stress: fast swings in residual demand push units toward their ramp limits and increase the risk of infeasibility.

\paragraph{A3: Peak-to-valley ratio (proxy).}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{A3}(s)=\mathrm{PTV},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{PTV}$ is a scenario-level proxy of the peak-to-valley ratio of net demand.

\paragraph{}A large peak-to-valley ratio means the system must cycle between low-load valleys (where units are decommitted to avoid minimum-generation violations) and high-load peaks (where all available capacity is needed). This amplifies the number of startup/shutdown transitions, each of which is a binary decision with associated costs. The wider the ratio, the more the solver must trade off startup costs against operating costs across the horizon.

\paragraph{A4: Short-term variability (weather-profile mapping).}
Let $\omega$ be a categorical weather label. The implementation maps $\omega$ to a scalar:
\begin{equation}
x_{A4}(s)=
\begin{cases}
0.08 & \omega=\texttt{calm\_winter},\\
0.22 & \omega=\texttt{stormy\_winter},\\
0.18 & \omega=\texttt{sunny\_summer},\\
0.06 & \omega=\texttt{overcast\_summer},\\
0.14 & \omega=\texttt{mixed},\\
0.12 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Weather profiles encode intra-day renewable variability patterns that are not captured by the aggregate VRE penetration ratio. Stormy winter conditions produce rapid wind ramps and cloud transients that create short-horizon supply shocks; sunny summer conditions cause steep solar ramps at sunrise and sunset. Criticality emerges from the mismatch between the timescale of these weather-driven fluctuations and the response time of dispatchable units (constrained by ramp limits and minimum up/down times).

\paragraph{A5: Demand scale factor.}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{A5}(s)=\lambda_D.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}The demand scale factor uniformly amplifies all zonal loads, pushing the system closer to its generation capacity limits. Criticality emerges because higher demand tightens the supply--demand balance, reduces available reserves, and forces the solver to commit marginal units whose on/off status has a significant impact on both cost and feasibility. At high $\lambda_D$, even small perturbations in renewable output can trigger unserved energy or costly emergency actions.

\paragraph{A6: Peak-to-firm capacity ratio (proxy).}
Using \eqref{eq:aux_peak_firm}, define:
\begin{equation}
x_{A6}(s)=\frac{D_{\mathrm{peak}}(s)}{\max\!\left(P_{\mathrm{firm}}(s),1\right)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}This ratio captures the system's \emph{adequacy margin}: when peak demand approaches or exceeds firm (dispatchable) capacity, the system has no room for error. Criticality emerges because a ratio near or above~1 means that every dispatchable unit must be committed at peak, leaving no reserves. The solver must then find the exact commitment schedule that threads the needle between adequacy and cost minimisation, with binary decisions becoming highly consequential.

\paragraph{A7: Inverse storage power adequacy (proxy).}
Let $P^{\mathrm{stor}}$ be total storage power (MW). Using \eqref{eq:aux_respeak}:
\begin{equation}
x_{A7}(s)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{P^{\mathrm{stor}}}{\max\!\left(D_{\mathrm{res,peak}}(s),1\right)},-1,1\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Storage power adequacy measures whether the installed storage fleet can inject enough power to cover residual-demand peaks. The metric is inverted (higher = less adequate) so that it increases with stress. Criticality emerges because insufficient storage power forces the solver to rely on slower, costlier flexibility resources (thermal ramping, demand response, cross-border imports) and to optimise the precise charge/discharge timing---a tightly coupled binary-and-continuous decision across the full horizon.

\paragraph{A8: Inverse storage energy adequacy (proxy).}
Let $E^{\mathrm{stor}}$ be total storage energy (MWh). Define a proxy for cumulative positive residual energy:
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{pos}}(s)=0.4\,H\,D_{\mathrm{res,peak}}(s),
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
x_{A8}(s)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{E^{\mathrm{stor}}}{\max\!\left(S_{\mathrm{pos}}(s),1\right)},-1,1\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}While A7 captures instantaneous power adequacy, A8 captures \emph{energy} adequacy: whether the storage fleet can absorb and release enough cumulative energy over the horizon. Criticality emerges because limited energy capacity forces the solver to optimise the temporal allocation of storage cycles precisely---charging too early or too late can leave the system without reserves when they are most needed. This creates strong inter-temporal coupling between storage SOC variables across all timesteps.

\paragraph{A9: Inverse thermal flexibility (proxy).}
Let $\rho_{\mathrm{ramp}}$ be the thermal ramp percentage parameter. Define the thermal flexibility proxy:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{th,flex}}(s)=n_{\mathrm{th}}\,\overline{P}_{\mathrm{th}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{ramp}},
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
x_{A9}(s)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{P_{\mathrm{th,flex}}(s)}{\max\!\left(D_{\mathrm{res,peak}}(s),1\right)},-1,1\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Thermal flexibility is the primary means of following residual-demand variations in conventional systems. The inverse metric increases when the fleet's aggregate ramping capability is small relative to the residual-demand peak. Criticality emerges because low thermal flexibility means that the solver cannot simply ramp existing committed units up or down; instead, it must commit or decommit additional units (binary decisions) to track demand, incurring startup costs and minimum-up/down-time constraints that tighten the feasible space.

\paragraph{A10: Inverse DR headroom (proxy).}
Let $P^{\mathrm{DR}}$ be total demand-response capacity (MW). Define a proxy for total energy demand over the horizon:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{tot}}(s)=n_Z\,\overline{D}_Z\,\lambda_D\,H,
\end{equation}
and a DR headroom proxy
\begin{equation}
h_{\mathrm{DR}}(s)=\frac{P^{\mathrm{DR}}H}{\max\!\left(D_{\mathrm{tot}}(s),1\right)}.
\end{equation}
Then the implemented metric is
\begin{equation}
x_{A10}(s)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(1-h_{\mathrm{DR}}(s),0,1\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Demand response provides a fast, binary flexibility resource (shed or not shed) that can substitute for costly generation commitment changes. When DR headroom is low (high $x_{A10}$), this flexibility cushion is unavailable, and the solver must rely entirely on supply-side adjustments. Criticality emerges because the loss of DR headroom eliminates a degree of freedom that would otherwise help the solver satisfy power-balance constraints during stress periods without committing additional thermal units.

\paragraph{A11: Trade reliance (cross-border policy proxy).}
Let $\pi_{\mathrm{xb}}\in\{\texttt{allow},\texttt{cap},\texttt{block}\}$ be the cross-border policy label and $n_{\mathrm{nb}}$ the number of neighbouring nations.
The code defines
\begin{equation}
\phi(\pi_{\mathrm{xb}})=
\begin{cases}
0.25 & \pi_{\mathrm{xb}}=\texttt{allow},\\
0.15 & \pi_{\mathrm{xb}}=\texttt{cap},\\
0.02 & \pi_{\mathrm{xb}}=\texttt{block},\\
0.15 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
and sets
\begin{equation}
x_{A11}(s)=\phi(\pi_{\mathrm{xb}})\cdot \min\!\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{nb}}}{4},1\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Trade reliance captures the extent to which the system depends on cross-border exchanges to balance supply and demand. High trade reliance (permissive cross-border policy with many neighbours) introduces import/export flow variables and associated binary mode decisions (import vs.\ export per zone per timestep), expanding the MILP's decision space. Criticality emerges because the solver must jointly optimise internal dispatch and cross-border flows, with the flow directions introducing additional combinatorial choices that couple zones spatially.

\paragraph{A12: Inverse congestion exposure (intertie density proxy).}
Let $\rho_{\mathrm{it}}$ be the intertie density parameter. The implemented proxy is
\begin{equation}
x_{A12}(s)=1-\rho_{\mathrm{it}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Intertie density measures the proportion of possible inter-regional transmission links that are actually built. The inverse metric increases when the network is sparse. Criticality emerges because a sparse network limits the system's ability to redistribute power across regions: local surpluses cannot be exported and local deficits cannot be relieved by imports, forcing each region to solve its own commitment problem almost independently. This creates congestion bottlenecks that tighten the feasible space and can push the solver toward expensive local solutions or unserved energy.

% ============================================================
% B) Combinatorial hardness metrics (B1--B10)
% ============================================================

\subsubsection*{B. Combinatorial hardness metrics (B1--B10)}

\paragraph{B1: Number of zones.}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{B1}(s)=n_Z.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}The number of zones is the most direct measure of problem scale. Each additional zone introduces its own set of generation, storage, and demand-response binary variables, its own power-balance constraint, and coupling constraints with neighbouring zones via transmission links. Criticality emerges because MILP solve time grows super-linearly with the number of zones: more zones mean more binary variables, more constraints, and a larger branch-and-bound tree.

\paragraph{B2: Horizon length in number of timesteps.}
Let $\Delta t$ be the time step length in minutes. The number of timesteps is
\begin{equation}
n_T=\left\lfloor \frac{60H}{\Delta t}\right\rfloor,
\end{equation}
and the implemented metric is
\begin{equation}
x_{B2}(s)=n_T.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}The horizon length multiplies the problem size along the temporal axis: each additional timestep replicates the full set of per-zone variables and constraints. Criticality emerges because longer horizons also strengthen inter-temporal coupling (storage SOC continuity, minimum up/down times, ramp limits carry state from one timestep to the next), making the LP relaxation weaker and branch-and-bound more expensive.

\paragraph{B3: Estimated number of binary variables.}
Let $\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{bin}}$ be read from \texttt{diff.n\_binary\_variables}; if missing, the code uses the fallback
$\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{bin}}=0.1\times\texttt{estimates.vars\_total}$. Then
\begin{equation}
x_{B3}(s)=\widehat{n}_{\mathrm{bin}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}The total number of binary variables is the single most predictive feature of MILP solve time in unit-commitment problems \parencite{morales_espana_tight_2013}. Each binary variable doubles the size of the feasible space that branch-and-bound must explore in the worst case. Criticality emerges directly: more binaries mean a combinatorially larger search tree and, empirically, longer solve times that often exceed solver time limits.

\paragraph{B4: Thermal unit density.}
Definition:
\begin{equation}
x_{B4}(s)=\frac{n_{\mathrm{th}}}{\max(n_Z,1)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Thermal unit density measures the number of individually committed thermal generators per zone. Unlike B3, which counts total binaries, B4 captures the \emph{density} of commitment decisions within each zone's sub-problem. Criticality emerges because dense thermal fleets create many near-equivalent commitment combinations (e.g., committing unit~A vs.\ unit~B at similar costs), producing a flat objective landscape that slows branch-and-bound convergence. This is the classic symmetry problem in unit-commitment MILP formulations.

\paragraph{B5: Minimum-generation tightness (constant proxy).}
The current implementation uses a constant proxy:
\begin{equation}
x_{B5}(s)=0.35.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Minimum-generation tightness measures the ratio $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}} / \overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}$ of the thermal fleet. A high ratio means committed units cannot reduce output below a significant fraction of their capacity, creating \emph{must-run} blocks that constrain the power balance. Criticality emerges because tight minimum-generation bounds force the solver to decommit units entirely (a binary decision) rather than simply reducing their output, increasing the number of on/off transitions. The current implementation uses a constant proxy pending per-unit data.

\paragraph{B6: Startup cost intensity (proxy).}
Define the total startup cost proxy
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{SU}}(s)=n_{\mathrm{th}}c^{\mathrm{SU}}_{\mathrm{th}}+n_{\mathrm{nuc}}c^{\mathrm{SU}}_{\mathrm{nuc}},
\end{equation}
and the variable-cost proxy over the horizon
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{var}}(s)=\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}\overline{P}_{\mathrm{th}}+n_{\mathrm{nuc}}\overline{P}_{\mathrm{nuc}}\right)\cdot H \cdot \kappa \cdot c_{\mathrm{var}}.
\end{equation}
Then the metric is
\begin{equation}
x_{B6}(s)=\min\!\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{SU}}(s)}{\max(C_{\mathrm{var}}(s),1)},\,0.5\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Startup cost intensity measures the ratio of fixed commitment costs (startup) to variable running costs. When startup costs are high relative to fuel costs, the on/off decision for each unit becomes economically consequential: committing a unit incurs a large sunk cost, but not committing it risks expensive alternatives (imports, DR, unserved energy). Criticality emerges because high startup intensity creates a \emph{non-convex} cost landscape where the LP relaxation significantly underestimates the true MILP cost, weakening the relaxation bound and slowing branch-and-bound convergence.

\paragraph{B7: Ramping tightness (proxy).}
Let $P_{\mathrm{th}}(s)=n_{\mathrm{th}}\overline{P}_{\mathrm{th}}$ be total thermal capacity (proxy) and
$R_{\mathrm{th}}(s)=P_{\mathrm{th}}(s)\rho_{\mathrm{ramp}}$ the corresponding ramping capability proxy.
The implemented ratio is
\begin{equation}
x_{B7}(s)=\frac{P_{\mathrm{th}}(s)}{\max(R_{\mathrm{th}}(s),1)}.
\end{equation}
(With these proxies, this simplifies to approximately $1/\rho_{\mathrm{ramp}}$ when $R_{\mathrm{th}}(s)\ge 1$.)

\paragraph{}Ramping tightness captures how constrained the thermal fleet is in adjusting output between consecutive timesteps. A high ratio (capacity $\gg$ ramp capability) means that units can only change output slowly, creating strong temporal coupling between commitment decisions: the solver must plan ahead to ensure that enough capacity is online \emph{before} demand rises. Criticality emerges because tight ramp limits interact with startup constraints to create multi-period commitment dependencies that the LP relaxation cannot capture, widening the integrality gap.

\paragraph{B8: Storage SOC tightness proxy.}
Let $h_{E/P}$ be the battery energy-to-power duration (hours) and $\tau_{\mathrm{SOC}}$ the final SOC tolerance. Then
\begin{equation}
x_{B8}(s)=\frac{h_{E/P}}{\max(\tau_{\mathrm{SOC}},0.01)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}SOC tightness measures the ratio of the battery's energy-to-power duration to the allowed SOC deviation at the end of the horizon. A high ratio means the storage system has deep energy reservoirs relative to its SOC tolerance, creating long-range temporal dependencies: the solver must plan charge/discharge cycles over many timesteps to ensure the final SOC constraint is met. Criticality emerges because the SOC continuity equation links every timestep's charge/discharge binary decision to the cumulative energy trajectory, making greedy or myopic strategies infeasible and forcing global optimisation across the full horizon.

\paragraph{B9: Interconnection density (derived proxy).}
Let $n_R$ be the number of regions and $\rho_{\mathrm{it}}$ the intertie density parameter. The code estimates the number of inter-regional links as
\begin{equation}
\widehat{n}_L(s)=\rho_{\mathrm{it}}\frac{n_R(n_R-1)}{2},
\end{equation}
and defines
\begin{equation}
x_{B9}(s)=\frac{\widehat{n}_L(s)}{\max(n_Z,1)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Interconnection density measures the number of inter-regional transmission links relative to the number of zones. Dense interconnection introduces more flow variables and transmission constraints (capacity limits, loss factors), expanding the LP's constraint matrix. Criticality emerges because each transmission link couples the power-balance constraints of two zones, creating a network of spatial dependencies. Dense networks prevent the MILP from decomposing into independent zonal sub-problems, forcing the solver to consider the full spatial coupling simultaneously.

\paragraph{B10: Network heterogeneity (zones-per-region dispersion).}
Let $\{Z_r\}_{r=1}^{n_R}$ be the number of zones in each region. The implemented heterogeneity proxy is
\begin{equation}
x_{B10}(s)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\mathrm{std}(Z_r)}{\max(\mathrm{mean}(Z_r),1)} & n_R>1,\\[4pt]
0 & n_R=1.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{}Network heterogeneity measures the dispersion (coefficient of variation) of zone counts across regions. A heterogeneous network contains both small regions (few zones, easy sub-problems) and large regions (many zones, hard sub-problems). Criticality emerges because the MILP's solve time is dominated by the hardest region: a single large, densely interconnected region can bottleneck the entire branch-and-bound process. Heterogeneity also challenges the EBM's learned representations, which must generalise across regions of very different sizes within the same scenario.

\paragraph{Remark on proxy nature.}
Several components are proxy-based because the generator does not necessarily store full time-series at metric-computation time (e.g., the categorical mapping in A4) and because some operational parameters are fixed in the current implementation (e.g., B5).
This appendix documents the \emph{exact} definitions used in the published code.

\section{Detailed MILP constraint formulation}
\label{app:milp_constraints}

This appendix provides the complete mathematical formulation of the nine constraint families summarized in Section~\ref{sec:milp_constraints}. All notation follows the definitions of Section~\ref{sec:milp_variables}.

\subsection{Thermal unit commitment}

Thermal output is bounded by the commitment binary and subject to symmetric inter-temporal ramp limits:
\begin{align}
\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z \, u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}
&\leq p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}
\leq \overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z \, u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t},
& \forall\, z,t, \label{eq:thermal_bounds} \\
p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} - p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{th}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1, \label{eq:thermal_ramp_up} \\
p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1} - p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{th}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1. \label{eq:thermal_ramp_down}
\end{align}
Here $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ and $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ denote the minimum and maximum stable generation levels, and $R^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ is the per-period ramp limit.
The startup indicator is linked to the commitment trajectory as:
\begin{equation}
v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \geq u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} - u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1},
\quad \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1.
\label{eq:thermal_startup}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Nuclear baseload}

Nuclear units are always-online baseload with limited modulation:
\begin{align}
\underline{P}^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z
&\leq p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t}
\leq \overline{P}^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z,
& \forall\, z,t, \label{eq:nuclear_bounds} \\
p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t} - p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t-1}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1, \label{eq:nuclear_ramp_up} \\
p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t-1} - p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1, \label{eq:nuclear_ramp_down}
\end{align}
with $R^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z = 0.5\,\overline{P}^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z$.

\subsection{Renewable generation and curtailment}

Solar and wind output equal available power minus curtailment:
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t} + s^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t} = \overline{P}^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t}, \quad
p^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t} + s^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t} = \overline{P}^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t}, \quad \forall\, z,t.
\label{eq:renewable_balance}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Demand response}

Total shed equals the sum of tiered blocks, each bounded by its capacity:
\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
= \textstyle\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} d^{\mathrm{dr},k}_{z,t}, \quad
d^{\mathrm{dr},k}_{z,t} \leq \overline{D}^{k}_{z,t}, \quad
d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} \leq \overline{D}_{z,t},
\quad \forall\, z,t,k.
\label{eq:dr_blocks}
\end{equation}

The activation binary $\delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}$ links discrete activation decisions to
continuous shedding through big-M constraints:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon \, \delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
\leq d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
\leq \overline{D}_{z,t} \, \delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t},
\quad \forall\, z,t,
\label{eq:dr_activation}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon > 0$ enforces a minimum non-zero shed whenever demand response is activated.
The total number of activation events per zone is capped:
\begin{equation}
\textstyle\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \delta^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
\leq N^{\mathrm{act}}_z,
\quad \forall\, z.
\label{eq:dr_max_activations}
\end{equation}

A rebound energy backlog tracks deferred demand and must be sufficiently cleared by the end
of the horizon:
\begin{equation}
e^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
= (1 - \gamma)\, e^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t-1}
+ \Delta t \, d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}, \quad
e^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,T-1} \leq \bar{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dr}},
\quad \forall\, z,t,
\label{eq:dr_rebound}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the rebound decay rate and $\bar{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{dr}}$ is the
end-of-horizon tolerance on deferred energy.

Inter-temporal ramping constraints smooth demand-response activation:
\begin{align}
d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} - d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t-1}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{dr}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1, \label{eq:dr_ramp_up} \\
d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t-1} - d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}
&\leq R^{\mathrm{dr}}_z,
& \forall\, z,\, t \geq 1. \label{eq:dr_ramp_down}
\end{align}

\subsection{Battery storage}

Charge and discharge are bounded by rated power and mutually exclusive via the mode binary:
\begin{align}
b^{+}_{z,t} &\leq \overline{B}_z \, \sigma^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t}, &
b^{-}_{z,t} &\leq \overline{B}_z \, (1 - \sigma^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t}), & \forall\, z,t.
\label{eq:battery_mode}
\end{align}
The state of charge evolves with charging and discharging efficiencies ($\eta^{+}, \eta^{-}$) and self-discharge retention~$\rho^{\mathrm{bat}}_z$:
\begin{equation}
e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} = \rho^{\mathrm{bat}}_z \, e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t-1} + \Delta t \bigl( \eta^{+} b^{+}_{z,t} - b^{-}_{z,t} / \eta^{-} \bigr), \quad
0 \leq e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} \leq \overline{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_z,
\label{eq:battery_soc}
\end{equation}
with final SOC bounded within a tolerance band around the initial level:
\begin{equation}
\underline{E}^{\mathrm{bat,fin}}_z \leq e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,T-1} \leq \overline{E}^{\mathrm{bat,fin}}_z.
\label{eq:battery_final}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reservoir hydro}

Hydro reservoirs receive natural inflows and may release power or spill excess water:
\begin{equation}
e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t} = e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t-1} + \Delta t \bigl( I^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t} - h^{\mathrm{rel}}_{z,t} - h^{\mathrm{spl}}_{z,t} \bigr), \quad
h^{\mathrm{rel}}_{z,t} \leq \overline{H}_z, \quad
e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t} \leq \overline{E}^{\mathrm{hyd}}_z.
\label{eq:hydro_reservoir}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Pumped-hydro storage}

Pumped-hydro mirrors the battery formulation with its own mode binary, efficiencies, retention factor, and final level bounds:
\begin{align}
g^{+}_{z,t} &\leq \overline{G}_z \, \sigma^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t}, &
g^{-}_{z,t} &\leq \overline{G}_z \, (1 - \sigma^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t}), & \forall\, z,t, \label{eq:pumped_mode} \\
e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t} &= \rho^{\mathrm{phs}}_z \, e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t-1} + \Delta t \bigl( \eta^{+}_{\mathrm{p}} g^{+}_{z,t} - g^{-}_{z,t} / \eta^{-}_{\mathrm{p}} \bigr), &
0 \leq e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t} &\leq \overline{E}^{\mathrm{phs}}_z. \label{eq:pumped_soc}
\end{align}
Final level bounds $\underline{E}^{\mathrm{phs,fin}}_z \leq e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,T-1} \leq \overline{E}^{\mathrm{phs,fin}}_z$ are enforced analogously to battery storage.

\subsection{Transmission network and cross-border exchanges}

Power flows on each line are bounded by capacity (bidirectional):
\begin{equation}
-\overline{F}_\ell \leq f_{\ell,t} \leq \overline{F}_\ell, \quad \forall\, \ell \in \mathcal{L},\, t \in \mathcal{T}.
\label{eq:transmission}
\end{equation}
Import and export are mutually exclusive via a binary mode variable and bounded by the aggregate cross-border capacity $\overline{M}$:
\begin{equation}
m^{+}_{t} \leq \overline{M} \, \sigma^{\mathrm{imp}}_{t}, \quad
m^{-}_{t} \leq \overline{M} \, (1 - \sigma^{\mathrm{imp}}_{t}), \quad \forall\, t.
\label{eq:import_export}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Nodal power balance}

At every zone and time step, supply must equal demand:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\underbrace{p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} + p^{\mathrm{nuc}}_{z,t} + p^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t}
+ p^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t} + h^{\mathrm{rel}}_{z,t} + p^{\mathrm{ror}}_{z,t}
+ b^{-}_{z,t} + g^{-}_{z,t}}_{\text{generation}}
&+ d^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t} + q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t}
+ \!\!\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{to}}_z}\!\! f_{\ell,t}
+ \mathbb{1}_{[z=z_0]}(m^{+}_{t} - m^{-}_{t}) \\
&= D_{z,t} + b^{+}_{z,t} + g^{+}_{z,t}
+ \!\!\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{from}}_z}\!\! f_{\ell,t}
+ q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:power_balance}
\end{equation}
where $D_{z,t}$ is the exogenous demand, $p^{\mathrm{ror}}_{z,t}$ is the must-take run-of-river hydro, $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{to}}_z$ and $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{from}}_z$ are lines arriving at and departing from zone~$z$, and $z_0$ is the designated anchor zone for cross-border exchanges.

\section{Graph schema and encoder architecture details}
\label{app:graph_hte_details}

This appendix provides the formal definitions, complete edge-type catalogue, and architectural equations summarised in Section~\ref{sec:graph_encoder}.

\subsection{Graph definition}
\label{app:graph_schema}

Each scenario~$s$ is represented as a heterogeneous attributed graph
$\mathcal{G}(s) = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \phi_V, \phi_E, \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{A})$,
where $\mathcal{V}$ is the node set, $\mathcal{E} \subseteq \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V}$ the edge set, $\phi_V : \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{C}_V$ assigns each node to one of five types, $\phi_E : \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{C}_E$ assigns each edge to one of eleven types, and $\mathbf{X}$, $\mathbf{A}$ collect typed node and edge feature vectors respectively.

\paragraph{Node types.}
\emph{Nation} ($|\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{nat}}|{=}1$, $\mathbb{R}^{10}$): system-wide aggregates.
\emph{Region} ($|\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{reg}}|{=}|\mathcal{R}|$, $\mathbb{R}^{10}$): regional capacities, weather metadata.
\emph{Zone} ($|\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{zon}}|{=}|\mathcal{Z}|$, $\mathbb{R}^{10}$): per-zone capacity, demand.
\emph{Asset} (variable count, $\mathbb{R}^{8}$): rated capacity, marginal cost, ramp rate, storage parameters, parent index; created only when capacity $>0$.
\emph{Weather} ($|\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{wth}}|{=}|\mathcal{R}|$, $\mathbb{R}^{5}$): spread intensity, profile encoding.
All vectors are zero-padded to $d_{\max}$ before batching.

\paragraph{Spatial edges (7 types).}
Containment: nation$\to$region, region$\to$zone, zone$\to$zonal asset, region$\to$regional asset.
Weather influence: weather$\to$zone, weather$\to$renewable asset.
Transmission: zone$\leftrightarrow$zone (bidirectional, attributes: capacity, distance).

\paragraph{Temporal extension.}
Each base node is replicated $T$ times; features are augmented with a sinusoidal positional encoding ($x^{\mathrm{supra}}_{(v,t)} = [x_v(t) \| \tau(t)]$).
Spatial edges are replicated at each time step. Four temporal edge families are added: SOC edges for storage (type~8), ramp edges for thermal/nuclear (type~9), DR cooldown edges (type~10), and temporal self-loops for stateful assets (type~7).

\subsection{Hierarchical Temporal Encoder: architecture and training details}
\label{app:hte_details}

\subsubsection*{Input projection}

Each supra-node feature vector $x^{\mathrm{supra}}_{(v,t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\max} + F_t}$ is projected to hidden dimension $D$:
\begin{equation}
h^{(0)}_{v,t} = W_{\mathrm{in}}\, x^{\mathrm{supra}}_{(v,t)} + b_{\mathrm{in}}, \qquad W_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times (d_{\max}+F_t)}.
\end{equation}
The result is reshaped into $H^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{base}} \times T \times D}$.

\subsubsection*{Phase~1: Bottom-up spatial encoding}

At each level $\ell \in \{\text{asset}, \text{zone}, \text{region}\}$, $L_s$ GATv2 layers~\citep{brody_how_2022} ($H$ heads, $D/H$ dim per head, no concatenation) update representations via:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{h}^{(l)} = \mathrm{GATv2}\!\bigl(h^{(l-1)},\, \mathcal{E}_\ell\bigr), \quad
h^{(l)} = h^{(l-1)} + \mathrm{Dropout}\!\bigl(\mathrm{GELU}\!\bigl(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\tilde{h}^{(l)})\bigr)\bigr).
\label{eq:gat_block}
\end{equation}
Edge sets $\mathcal{E}_\ell$: full supra-graph at asset level, intra-region FC at zone level, all-to-all at region level. After each block, a skip connection is saved and mean-pooling aggregates to the next coarser level:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{pool}}_{c,t} = \frac{1}{|\pi^{-1}(c)|} \sum_{v \in \pi^{-1}(c)} h^{(L_s)}_{v,t}.
\label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
A final mean over regions yields $H_{\mathrm{nat}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times T \times D}$.

\subsubsection*{Phase~2: Temporal Transformer}

A Transformer encoder~\citep{vaswani2017attention} ($L_t$ layers, $H$ heads, FFN width $4D$, sinusoidal positional encoding):
\begin{equation}
H^{\mathrm{temp}}_{\mathrm{nat}} = \mathrm{TransformerEncoder}\!\bigl(\mathrm{PosEnc}(H_{\mathrm{nat}})\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times T \times D}.
\label{eq:temporal_transformer}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Phase~3: Top-down decoding}

Skip connections and GATv2 decoders propagate the nation representation back:
\begin{align}
H^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{reg}} &= \mathrm{GATv2}_{\mathrm{reg}}^{\mathrm{dec}}\!\Bigl(\mathrm{broadcast}(H^{\mathrm{temp}}_{\mathrm{nat}}) + H^{\mathrm{skip}}_{\mathrm{reg}}\Bigr), \label{eq:topdown_reg} \\
H^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{zon}} &= \mathrm{GATv2}_{\mathrm{zon}}^{\mathrm{dec}}\!\Bigl(\mathrm{unpool}_{\pi_{\mathrm{z \to r}}}(H^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{reg}}) + H^{\mathrm{skip}}_{\mathrm{zon}}\Bigr), \label{eq:topdown_zon} \\
H^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{ast}} &= \mathrm{GATv2}_{\mathrm{ast}}^{\mathrm{dec}}\!\Bigl(\mathrm{unpool}_{\pi_{\mathrm{a \to z}}}(H^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\mathrm{zon}}) + H^{\mathrm{skip}}_{\mathrm{ast}}\Bigr), \label{eq:topdown_ast}
\end{align}
where $h^{\mathrm{unpool}}_{v,t} = h^{\mathrm{dec}}_{\pi(v),t}$ indexes into the parent embedding.

\subsubsection*{Output}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HTE}(s) = \bigl\{
  H_{\mathrm{ast}} \!\in\! \mathbb{R}^{N_a \times T \times D},\;
  H_{\mathrm{zon}} \!\in\! \mathbb{R}^{N_z \times T \times D},\;
  H_{\mathrm{reg}} \!\in\! \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times T \times D},\;
  H_{\mathrm{nat}} \!\in\! \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}
\bigr\}.
\label{eq:hte_output}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Multi-lag InfoNCE loss}

Anchor--positive pairs at lags $\Lambda = \{1, 4, 8\}$ on $\ell_2$-normalised asset embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(a,p) \in \mathcal{P}} \log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(a, p) / \kappa)}{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(a, p) / \kappa) + \sum_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{neg}}} \exp(\mathrm{sim}(a, n_j) / \kappa)},
\label{eq:infonce}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sim}(a, b) = a^\top b / (\|a\|\|b\|)$ and $\kappa$ is a temperature parameter.

\section{Latent UC stack: architecture and training details}
\label{app:ebm_details}

This appendix details the three modules of the latent UC stack summarised in Section~\ref{sec:ebm}: the UC autoencoder (Appendix~\ref{app:uc_ae}), the UC-JEPA scenario-to-latent predictor (Appendix~\ref{app:uc_jepa_arch}), the latent EBM and its Langevin sampler (Appendix~\ref{app:latent_ebm_arch}), and the joint hyperparameter table (Appendix~\ref{app:latent_stack_hp}).

\subsection{UC autoencoder}
\label{app:uc_ae}

\paragraph{Encoder.}
For a binary tensor $u \in \{0,1\}^{B \times Z \times T \times F}$ with zone mask $m \in \{0,1\}^{B\times Z}$, the encoder first projects each per-(zone, time) feature vector with a linear map of width $H$, then runs an $L_g$-layer bidirectional GRU along $T$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde u_{z,t} = \mathrm{BiGRU}\bigl(W_{\mathrm{in}}\,u_{z,t} + b_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr)_t \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{2H}.
\end{equation}
Zone embeddings are obtained by mean-pooling along $T$ and projecting back to $H$: $e_z = W_p\,\overline{\tilde u}_z$. A set of $K$ learned query tokens $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times H}$ cross-attends over the (masked) zone embeddings, with padded zones excluded via a key-padding mask, and a linear head produces the latent:
\begin{equation}
z_{uc} = W_{\mathrm{lat}}\;\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\bigl(Q + \mathrm{MHA}(Q, \{e_z\}, \{e_z\};\,m)\bigr) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}.
\label{eq:uc_ae_enc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Decoder.}
The decoder reads $z_{uc}$, builds learned (zone, time) queries from positional embeddings $e_z^{\mathrm{pos}} + e_t^{\mathrm{pos}}$, optionally adds the projected scenario embedding $h_{z,t}$ as a per-(zone, time) bias, and applies $L_d$ blocks of (cross-attention onto $z_{uc}$) + (FFN) before a final linear head $W_{\mathrm{out}}: \mathbb{R}^H \to \mathbb{R}^F$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{logits}_{z,t} = W_{\mathrm{out}}\;\mathrm{Block}^{L_d}\!\bigl(e_z^{\mathrm{pos}} + e_t^{\mathrm{pos}} + \pi_h(h_{z,t});\;z_{uc}\bigr).
\label{eq:uc_ae_dec}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Loss.}
With masked BCE per feature, the AE training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AE}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}^{\mathrm{pw}} + \lambda_t\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}} + \lambda_c\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{su}},
\end{equation}
where the per-feature positive weights are $w_f = \mathrm{clip}\bigl((1-p_f)/p_f,\,1,\,w_{\max}\bigr)$ with $p_f$ the empirical positive rate of feature $f$, and the auxiliary regularisers expand to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}} = \mathbb{E}_m\bigl[|p_{z,t+1,f} - p_{z,t,f}|\bigr], \quad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{su}} = \mathbb{E}_m\bigl[\bigl(v^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} - \mathrm{ReLU}(u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} - u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t-1})\bigr)^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
with $p = \sigma(\mathrm{logits})$ and $\mathbb{E}_m$ a mean over the entries of the broadcast mask.

\subsection{UC-JEPA scenario-to-latent predictor}
\label{app:uc_jepa_arch}

\paragraph{Shared context trunk.}
The predictor receives $h \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times Z\times T\times D}$ and the zone mask $m$. A linear projection $W_{\mathrm{in}}^J: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^H$ and an $L_g$-layer BiGRU produce per-(zone, time) hidden states $\hat h_{z,t} \in \mathbb{R}^H$. Three context tensors are derived: a per-(zone, time) tensor $\tilde h_{z,t}$, a zone summary $\bar h_z = \mathrm{mean}_t \tilde h_{z,t}$, and a system-time summary $\bar h_t = \mathrm{masked\_mean}_z \tilde h_{z,t}$.

\paragraph{Latent head.}
$K$ learned queries $Q^J$ cross-attend over $\{\bar h_z\}$ with the zone mask:
\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{anc}} = W_{\mathrm{lat}}^J\;\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\bigl(Q^J + \mathrm{MHA}(Q^J, \{\bar h_z\}, \{\bar h_z\};\,m)\bigr) + b_{\mathrm{base}},
\label{eq:jepa_lat}
\end{equation}
where $b_{\mathrm{base}} \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times d}$ is an optional learned residual base (a constant offset that empirically speeds up convergence).

\paragraph{Auxiliary UC heads.}
When enabled, five auxiliary linear heads operate on the per-(zone, time) hidden $\tilde h_{z,t}$ and the system-time hidden $\bar h_t$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Storage modes (battery + pumped, 4 logits) and DR (1 logit): $W^{\mathrm{stor}}\tilde h_{z,t}$, $W^{\mathrm{dr}}\tilde h_{z,t}$.
  \item Thermal commitment (1 logit): $W^{\mathrm{on}}\tilde h_{z,t}$.
  \item Thermal start-up: either a learned head $W^{\mathrm{su}}\tilde h_{z,t}$ or the deterministic derivation $\mathrm{logit}(\mathrm{ReLU}(p^{\mathrm{on}}_{z,t} - p^{\mathrm{on}}_{z,t-1}))$ that mirrors the AE start-up consistency.
  \item Import mode (1 logit per time, broadcast over $z$): $W^{\mathrm{imp}}\bar h_t$.
\end{itemize}
The concatenation of these eight logits, in the canonical UC feature order, defines the auxiliary tensor $\mathrm{aux}_{z,t} \in \mathbb{R}^F$.

\paragraph{Loss.}
With stop-gradient on the AE encoder output and a small Gaussian noise on the target,
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JEPA}}
   &= w_m\,\big\|z_{\mathrm{anc}} - \mathrm{sg}(z^\star_{uc} + \epsilon)\big\|_2^2
   + w_v\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}(z_{\mathrm{anc}})
   + w_c\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cov}}(z_{\mathrm{anc}}) \notag \\
   &\quad + w_u\,\mathrm{Focal}\!\bigl(u^\star,\,\mathrm{UCDec}(z_{\mathrm{anc}}, h);\,\gamma_f, w_f\bigr)
   + w_a\,\mathrm{Focal}\!\bigl(u^\star,\,\mathrm{aux};\,\gamma_f, w_f\bigr),
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{Focal}(\cdot;\gamma_f, w_f)$ is the masked binary focal loss with per-feature positive weights $w_f$ and focal exponent $\gamma_f$, and the variance / covariance regularisers act per latent dimension $d$ (after centring and standardising along the batch axis).

\paragraph{Threshold tuning.}
At the end of JEPA training, per-feature thresholds $\boldsymbol\tau$ are searched on a held-out validation split by sweeping a grid $\mathcal{T} = \{0.20, 0.25, \dots, 0.80\}$ and selecting, for each feature $f$, the value that maximises the masked $F_1$ between $\mathbb{1}[\hat p_{\cdot,\cdot,f} \geq \tau]$ and $u^\star_{\cdot,\cdot,f}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:learned_threshold}). The resulting vector is stored in the checkpoint and reused at inference.

\subsection{Latent EBM and Langevin sampler}
\label{app:latent_ebm_arch}

\paragraph{Energy function.}
For a candidate latent $z \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times K\times d}$ (optionally with a sample axis $N$), the anchor $z_{\mathrm{anc}}$ and the pooled scenario context $\bar h \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times D}$ (masked mean of $h$ over zones and time), the energy is built in three steps:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{tok}_k     &= \mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{tok}}\bigl([z_k,\,z_{\mathrm{anc},k},\,z_k - z_{\mathrm{anc},k},\,z_k \odot z_{\mathrm{anc},k}]\bigr), \\
\mathrm{ctx}       &= \mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{ctx}}\bigl([\mathrm{mean}_k z_{\mathrm{anc}},\,\mathrm{std}_k z_{\mathrm{anc}},\,\bar h]\bigr), \\
E_\phi^{\mathrm{raw}} &= W_e\bigl[\mathrm{mean}_k \mathrm{tok},\,\mathrm{max}_k \mathrm{tok},\,\mathrm{ctx},\,\|z\|_2^2,\,\|z - z_{\mathrm{anc}}\|_2^2,\,\cos(z, z_{\mathrm{anc}})\bigr],
\end{align}
and the final energy is bounded by $E_\phi = E_{\max}\tanh(E_\phi^{\mathrm{raw}} / E_{\max})$. Both MLPs use $\mathrm{LayerNorm} \to \mathrm{Linear} \to \mathrm{GELU} \to \mathrm{Dropout} \to \mathrm{Linear} \to \mathrm{GELU}$.

\paragraph{Loss.}
With positives $z^\star_{uc} = \mathrm{UCEnc}(u^\star)$ and negatives $z^-$ from short Langevin chains seeded near $z_{\mathrm{anc}}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LEBM}} = \overline{E}_\phi(z^\star_{uc}) - \overline{E}_\phi(z^-) + \alpha_{\mathrm{reg}}\Bigl([|\overline{E}_\phi(z^\star_{uc})| - E_{\mathrm{soft}}]_+^2 + [|\overline{E}_\phi(z^-)| - E_{\mathrm{soft}}]_+^2\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where overlines denote masked batch means and $E_{\mathrm{soft}} < E_{\max}$ activates only when energies drift towards the bound.

\paragraph{Latent Langevin sampler.}
With cosine-annealed step size $\eta_k = \eta_0\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\pi k/(K-1))\bigr)$ and noise scale $\sigma_k = \sigma_0\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(\pi k/(K-1))\bigr)$, the update is
\begin{equation}
z^{(k+1)} = z^{(k)} - \eta_k\,\widehat{\nabla_z E_\phi} - \eta_k\,\lambda_d\,(z^{(k)} - z_{\mathrm{anc}}) + \sqrt{2\eta_k T}\,\sigma_k\,\varepsilon^{(k)},
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{\nabla_z E_\phi}$ the per-sample unit-norm gradient and $T$ a fixed Langevin temperature. The chain is initialised by one of four modes (\texttt{anchor}, \texttt{anchor\_noise}, \texttt{prior}, \texttt{mixed}); the prior $\mathcal{N}(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma)$ is estimated once on the training set as the per-token empirical mean and standard deviation of $\mathrm{UCEnc}(u^\star)$. Final latents are clipped to $[-c, c]^{K\times d}$ to prevent drift, and any non-finite energies encountered during the chain are replaced by the bound $\pm E_{\max}$.

\section{Pass-through warm-start decoder and cascading LP worker details}
\label{app:reconstruction_details}

This appendix provides the complete algorithmic specification of the pass-through warm-start decoder and the cascading LP worker summarised in Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}.

\subsection{Scenario physics}

The decoder operates on a \texttt{ScenarioPhysics} dataclass that encapsulates all physical and operational parameters needed to produce a feasible dispatch. The key fields are:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Topology:} number of zones~$Z$, timesteps~$T$, regions, zone-to-region mapping, anchor zone index.
  \item \textbf{Time series} $[Z \times T]$: demand $D_{z,t}$, solar available $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{sol}}_{z,t}$, wind available $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{wnd}}_{z,t}$, run-of-river hydro $p^{\mathrm{ror}}_{z,t}$, hydro inflow $I^{\mathrm{hyd}}_{z,t}$.
  \item \textbf{Per-zone scalars} $[Z]$: battery power $\overline{B}_z$ and capacity $\overline{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_z$, pumped-hydro power $\overline{G}_z$ and capacity $\overline{E}^{\mathrm{phs}}_z$, thermal capacity $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ and minimum $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$, nuclear capacity $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{nuc}}_z$, hydro reservoir capacity (MW and MWh), DR capacity.
  \item \textbf{Storage efficiencies:} separate charge/discharge efficiencies $\eta^+_{\mathrm{bat}}, \eta^-_{\mathrm{bat}}, \eta^+_{\mathrm{phs}}, \eta^-_{\mathrm{phs}}$ and self-discharge retention rates $\rho^{\mathrm{bat}}_z, \rho^{\mathrm{phs}}_z$.
  \item \textbf{Thermal dynamics:} ramp limit $R^{\mathrm{th}}_z$, initial output $p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,0}$, startup cost, marginal cost.
  \item \textbf{Initial states:} battery SOC, pumped-hydro level, hydro reservoir level (all as fractions of capacity).
\end{itemize}

These fields are populated from the scenario JSON via the MILP scenario loader, ensuring exact consistency with the data used by the MILP oracle.

\subsection{Pass-through warm-start algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:decoder} provides the complete pseudocode. The decoder returns a \texttt{FeasiblePlan} that bundles the input binaries $\hat{u}$ (unchanged) and a physically consistent continuous warm-start $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ covering all resource types. The plan is consumed by the LP worker only as an initial primal solution: the LP solver re-fixes the binaries to $\hat{u}$ and uses $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ to seed Pyomo \texttt{.value} attributes that APPSI--HiGHS reads through \texttt{setSolution()} for a crash basis.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Pass-through warm-start decoder}
\label{alg:decoder}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Binary candidate $\hat{u} \in \{0,1\}^{Z \times T \times F}$ ($F{=}8$), scenario physics $\mathcal{P}$
\Ensure Continuous warm-start $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$ (dispatch and storage states); binaries $\hat{u}$ unchanged

\State Initialise SOC: $e^{\mathrm{bat}}_z \gets \mathrm{SOC}_0^{\mathrm{bat}} \cdot \overline{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_z$, \; $e^{\mathrm{phs}}_z \gets \mathrm{SOC}_0^{\mathrm{phs}} \cdot \overline{E}^{\mathrm{phs}}_z$, \; $e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_z \gets E_0^{\mathrm{hyd}}$
\State Initialise $p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z \gets p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,\mathrm{init}}$, \; $h^{\mathrm{dr}}_z \gets 0$ \Comment{Thermal history, DR hours used}

\For{$t = 0, \dots, T{-}1$}
  \State Update hydro: $e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_z \gets \min(e^{\mathrm{hyd}}_z + I_{z,t} \Delta t, \, \overline{E}^{\mathrm{hyd}}_z)$
  \State Apply self-discharge: $e^{\mathrm{bat}}_z \gets \rho^{\mathrm{bat}}_z \cdot e^{\mathrm{bat}}_z$, \; $e^{\mathrm{phs}}_z \gets \rho^{\mathrm{phs}}_z \cdot e^{\mathrm{phs}}_z$
  \State Dispatch nuclear must-run, solar, wind, run-of-river; $r_z \gets D_{z,t} - \text{must-run}$ \Comment{Phase 1}

  \For{each zone $z$ with $r_z > 0$} \Comment{Phase 2: Deficit, binaries-respecting}
    \State Dispatch nuclear headroom and hydro reservoir (always available)
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{p,dis}}_{z,t}{=}1$: pumped discharge $\leq$ power and SOC
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{b,dis}}_{z,t}{=}1$: battery discharge $\leq$ power and SOC
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}{=}1$: thermal dispatch $\in [\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z, \min(\overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z, p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z + R^{\mathrm{th}}_z)]$
    \State Imports (capped by $\overline{P}^{\mathrm{imp}}$)
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{dr}}_{z,t}{=}1$ and $h^{\mathrm{dr}}_z < h^{\mathrm{dr}}_{\max}$: demand response
    \State Remaining residual $\to$ unserved energy
  \EndFor

  \For{each zone $z$ with $r_z < 0$} \Comment{Phase 3: Surplus, binaries-respecting}
    \State Export (anchor zone only), reduce thermal subject to ramp-down and $\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$
    \State Curtail wind, then solar
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{p,ch}}_{z,t}{=}1$: pumped charge $\leq$ power and headroom
    \State If $\hat{u}^{\mathrm{b,ch}}_{z,t}{=}1$: battery charge $\leq$ power and headroom
    \State Spill any remaining overgeneration
  \EndFor

  \State $p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z \gets p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t}$ \Comment{Update thermal history}
  \State $e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t+1} \gets e^{\mathrm{bat}}_z$, \; $e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t+1} \gets e^{\mathrm{phs}}_z$
\EndFor

\State \Return \texttt{FeasiblePlan}($\hat{u}$, $x_{\mathrm{ws}}$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{No fallback.}
In contrast with earlier prototypes, the warm-start decoder never flips a binary: if $\hat{u}$ flags a resource OFF, the corresponding continuous variable stays at zero and the resulting imbalance is absorbed by unserved energy or overgeneration. Any residual infeasibility is therefore visible to the LP worker (Section~\ref{sec:lp_worker}) and either resolved by Stage~1's relaxation of the unused integer modes or escalated to Stages~2--3.

\paragraph{Thermal ramp enforcement.}
When dispatching thermal generation at time~$t$, the maximum output is clamped to:
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \leq \min\!\bigl(\overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z,\; p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z + R^{\mathrm{th}}_z\bigr).
\label{eq:decoder_ramp}
\end{equation}
A startup exception applies: if $p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z = 0$ (unit was off), the decoder allows $p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = \underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z$ even if this exceeds the ramp limit, matching the MILP's implicit startup behaviour. Similarly, ramp-down is enforced during surplus handling:
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} \geq \max\!\bigl(\underline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z,\; p^{\mathrm{th,prev}}_z - R^{\mathrm{th}}_z\bigr) \quad \text{if } u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 1.
\label{eq:decoder_ramp_down}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Storage SOC tracking.}
At each time step, the SOC is updated using the MILP-consistent dynamics:
\begin{align}
e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t+1} &= \rho^{\mathrm{bat}}_z \, e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} + \Delta t \bigl(\eta^+_{\mathrm{bat}} \, b^+_{z,t} - b^-_{z,t} / \eta^-_{\mathrm{bat}}\bigr), \label{eq:decoder_soc_bat} \\
e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t+1} &= \rho^{\mathrm{phs}}_z \, e^{\mathrm{phs}}_{z,t} + \Delta t \bigl(\eta^+_{\mathrm{phs}} \, g^+_{z,t} - g^-_{z,t} / \eta^-_{\mathrm{phs}}\bigr). \label{eq:decoder_soc_phs}
\end{align}
Charge and discharge are bounded by both the rated power and the available headroom/energy:
\begin{equation}
b^+_{z,t} \leq \min\!\Bigl(\overline{B}_z, \; \frac{\overline{E}^{\mathrm{bat}}_z - e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t}}{\eta^+_{\mathrm{bat}} \, \Delta t}\Bigr), \qquad
b^-_{z,t} \leq \min\!\Bigl(\overline{B}_z, \; \frac{e^{\mathrm{bat}}_{z,t} \, \eta^-_{\mathrm{bat}}}{\Delta t}\Bigr).
\label{eq:decoder_storage_bounds}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Demand-response accounting.}
A running counter $h^{\mathrm{dr}}_z$ tracks the cumulative DR activation duration per zone. DR is only dispatched if $h^{\mathrm{dr}}_z < h^{\mathrm{dr}}_{\max}$ (default: 4 hours), after which the zone's DR capacity is exhausted for the remainder of the horizon.


\subsection{LP worker: deviation penalty formulation}

The LP worker builds on the full MILP model (Section~\ref{sec:milp_objective}--\ref{sec:milp_constraints}) with the following modifications at repair stages.

\paragraph{Deviation variables.}
For each (zone, time) pair $(z, t)$ in the unfixed subset $\mathcal{S}$, auxiliary non-negative variables $d^+_{z,t}, d^-_{z,t} \geq 0$ are introduced for each binary family $u \in \{u^{\mathrm{th}}, \sigma^{\mathrm{bat}}, \sigma^{\mathrm{phs}}, \delta^{\mathrm{dr}}\}$:
\begin{equation}
u_{z,t} - \hat{u}_{z,t} = d^+_{z,t} - d^-_{z,t}, \quad \forall\, (z,t) \in \mathcal{S},
\label{eq:lp_deviation}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{u}_{z,t}$ is the decoder's target value.

\paragraph{Penalised objective.}
The original UC objective $f(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{u})$ is augmented:
\begin{equation}
\min \; f(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{u}) + \lambda \!\!\sum_{(z,t) \in \mathcal{S}} \bigl(d^+_{z,t} + d^-_{z,t}\bigr),
\label{eq:lp_penalised}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = 10\,000$ for thermal commitment (the dominant binary family) and $\lambda/2$ for storage modes and DR activation. The high penalty ensures that deviations occur only when necessary for feasibility.

\paragraph{Flip budget constraint.}
\begin{equation}
\sum_{(z,t) \in \mathcal{S}} \bigl(d^+_{z,t} + d^-_{z,t}\bigr) \leq B,
\label{eq:lp_flip_budget}
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the stage-dependent budget (10\% or 50\% of total binaries). Since each binary is $\{0,1\}$ and each target is $\{0,1\}$, a single flip costs exactly~1, so $B$ directly limits the number of changed decisions.

\paragraph{True objective recovery.}
After solving, the LP worker reports the \emph{original} UC objective $f(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{u})$ (without the penalty term) as the solution cost. The penalty term is logged separately for diagnostics but is not included in the reported objective, ensuring fair comparison with the MILP oracle.


\subsection{Critical index selection}

The heuristic for selecting which $(z, t)$ pairs to unfix in Stages~2--3 uses a composite score:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(z, t) = 2\bigl(q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t} + q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t}\bigr) + \mathrm{tension}(z, t),
\label{eq:critical_score}
\end{equation}
where $q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t}$ and $q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t}$ are the unserved energy and overgeneration from the previous stage's solution, and the tension term captures thermal capacity mismatch:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{tension}(z, t) = \begin{cases}
0.5\,\overline{P}^{\mathrm{th}}_z & \text{if } q^{\mathrm{uns}}_{z,t} > 0 \text{ and } u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 0 \text{ but } \hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 1, \\
0.5\,p^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} & \text{if } q^{\mathrm{ovg}}_{z,t} > 0 \text{ and } u^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 1 \text{ but } \hat{u}^{\mathrm{th}}_{z,t} = 0, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tension}
\end{equation}
The top-$K$ pairs are selected, and each is expanded by a temporal window ($\pm 1$ time step) to capture inter-temporal coupling effects (ramp constraints, SOC continuity).
